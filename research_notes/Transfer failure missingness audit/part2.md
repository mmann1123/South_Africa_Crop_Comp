# Transfer failure missingness audit, part 2 of 2

Question for each paper: could missing or cloudy observations, handled differently or occurring at different rates in source and target, plausibly contribute to the reported cross-region transfer failure, in the way a zero-filled September did in our Western Cape holdout? Verdict scale: "likely", "possible", "unlikely". Where I could not check something it says "not verified". Sources I read directly are named in each section. Code was read at the commit hashes given.

Overall result: none of the seven papers reaches "likely". Three are "possible" (Orynbaikyzy 2022, Valinho 2024, Chen & Liu 2021). In each, the handling or the acquisition pattern leaves room for a missingness contribution, but none reports the per-region statistics that would show it. Four are "unlikely" or not applicable. The one code-level match to our mechanism is in Kerner et al. 2020 (togo-crop-mask). Its inference path writes a raw 0 into NaNs before the training normalizer. That paper does not report a transfer failure, though, and its compositing makes NaNs rare.

## 1. Rusňák et al. 2023, Remote Sensing 15(13):3414

**(1) Claimed transfer drop and availability.** The study covers Slovakia, Danubian Lowland to East Slovakian Lowland, with Sentinel-2 L2A for 2018 to 2022, April to August. Each pixel has five monthly median composites of ten bands, giving 50 features. The models are QDA, SVM, shallow NN and RF from MATLAB Classification Learner. Under spatial transfer with single-year source data (Scenario 3, Table 8) the mean OA is QDA 85.43, SVM 87.31, NN 87.02 and RF 81.62. RF falls to 72.10 in 2022. With five-year source data (Scenario 4, Table 9) RF reaches 85.28 and SVM 90.18. In-region same-year scores are not tabulated ("data not shown"), so the drop can only be read against the temporal holdout. The temporal-to-spatial drop is about 5.3 to 6.6 OA points (briefer, Tables 4, 5, 8). Reference data are public at https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.7912749 (Data Availability Statement). No code is released (not found).

**(2) Missing-data handling.** Section 2.3.1 says L2A cloud, cloud-shadow and cirrus pixels were masked using the SCL product. A per-pixel, per-band monthly median was then computed in GEE. There is no interpolation and no fill is described. The grid is fixed at five months, and the same features go to all four models. What happens to a pixel with zero valid observations in a month is not stated. Section 2.3.2 says fewer pixels per class could be extracted in 2020 and 2022 "due to observational limitations". That suggests such pixels were not sampled, but it is not verified.

**(3) Does missingness differ between regions?** Yes, somewhat, and the paper reports it. Section 3.1 and Figure 4 give the share of coverage with 1, 2, or 3 or more observations for every region, month and year. They highlight region-specific poor months: May 2019, June 2020 and May 2021. Section 4.2 tests whether observation quality explains transfer performance and finds "no coherent relationship". For example, May 2019 was poor in both regions, yet the 2019 spatial transfer was acceptable.

**(4) Verdict: unlikely.** Gaps are handled by a cloud-masked median that is identical in both regions. Nothing is filled with a constant, and the authors explicitly checked observation quality against transfer loss and found nothing. The RF-specific weakness is larger in the 2022 drought year, which points to a phenology and distribution shift rather than a data gap. *Data check:* for the sampled pixels, count months with zero valid observations per region and year (are there any?), and correlate per-pixel observation counts with per-pixel error in Scenario 3.

## 2. Orynbaikyzy, Gessner & Conrad 2022, Remote Sensing 14(6):1493

Read from the DLR copy, https://elib.dlr.de/186507/1/Orynbaikyzy_etal_2022.pdf. MDPI returned 403.

**(1) Claimed transfer drop and availability.** Seven German sites, 11 crops, 2018 reference data. Sentinel-1 and Sentinel-2 data cover 1 October 2017 to 31 October 2018. The study has 679 S2 scenes and 3,709 S1 scenes (Section 2.3). The model is Random Forest, tested with and without group-wise forward feature selection plus spatial CV. The protocol is 7-fold leave-one-site-out. "F1 loss" is the reference-site F1 minus the target-site F1 (Section 3.3). The median F1 loss is −0.04 for S1, −0.08 for S2 and −0.06 for S1+S2. Feature selection reduces the S1+S2 loss to about 0.04 (Section 4, text around Figure 7). The largest average losses are in target tiles HE, BB and BW, at −0.09, and the smallest in BY, at −0.01. The maximum F1 is 0.85 in training sites and 0.79 in unseen sites (Abstract). I found no data or code availability statement in the extracted text (not verified). The supplementary material is on MDPI.

**(2) Missing-data handling.** S2 was processed with MAJA 3.3 FRE products (Section 2.3). The text does not state explicitly that the MAJA cloud mask was applied, though "clouds and cloud shadows reduce the consistency of the time-series" implies it (not verified). Section 2.4 describes how the S2 series was regularised. The authors built 29 bi-weekly anchor dates and "interpolated nearest (on time dimension) observation values from optical data" onto them, which amounts to a nearest-neighbour fill in time. S1 uses a median over −7 to +6 days around each anchor. All sites go through the same procedure. There is no constant fill and no mask is given to the model. The RF receives the per-anchor values as features. Normalization is not relevant for RF.

**(3) Does missingness differ between regions?** It plausibly does. Section 2.4 says the sites "are located in different orbit tracks, resulting in variation of data acquisition times across sites". In the discussion (Section 5), the authors attribute S2's larger transfer loss partly to the weather dependence of valid optical observations, and say the outcome "could be the opposite" in areas without persistent cloud. Figure 2 shows precipitation frequency per site, and BW is flagged as wet. No per-site count of clear observations and no per-anchor gap length is reported.

**(4) Verdict: possible.** Nearest-in-time filling puts in-distribution values on the grid, unlike a zero fill. But at a cloudier site, an anchor in winter or spring may carry a value copied from weeks away, and the pattern of stale anchors would differ between the six pooled training sites and the held-out site. The authors themselves raise optical availability as an explanation for the S2-versus-S1 gap. The losses are small, though (0.01 to 0.09 F1), and phenology and field-size differences across Germany are equally plausible. *Data check:* for each site and anchor, compute the distribution of |anchor date − date of the nearest clear observation used|. Then test whether the per-site gap burden (HE, BB, BW versus BY) tracks the per-site S2 F1 loss, and whether the S2 minus S1 loss difference grows with it.

## 3. Xie, Zhang, Liu & Sun 2025, Int. J. Digital Earth 18(2):2563211

The briefer "Investigation of the spatio-temporal transferability of large-scale random forest crop classification models and potential influencing factors" is the same paper (DOI 10.1080/17538947.2025.2563211, verified in the PDF).

**(1) Claimed transfer drop and availability.** This is binary corn-versus-wheat classification with RF (500 trees). Training uses CDL labels in Kansas, South Dakota and Minnesota for 2019 to 2023. The model is applied to six European countries in 2018 and validated on LUCAS 2018. Inputs are Landsat 8 and Sentinel-2 harmonised: 13 layers times 8 monthly composites from March to October, for 104 features. The headline "up to 21.6%" (Abstract and conclusion) refers to the spread across source states. Section 3.3 gives 21.5 (Germany), 9.0, 15.2, 11.1, 1.0 and 11.9 OA points for 2019 models. Kansas is best and South Dakota worst. The transferred-minus-local gap is only 0.5 to 2.0 OA points (Fig. 5). Training and validation data are on figshare at https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.29925809.v1, and inputs are GEE asset IDs (Data availability statement). No code was found.

**(2) Missing-data handling.** Section 2.2.2, "Gap-free monthly time series satellite data construction", describes the steps: cloud mask with QA_PIXEL (Landsat) and QA60 (S2), then a per-pixel monthly median over all valid observations from both sensors. Remaining monthly gaps are filled by linear interpolation between the nearest valid months before and after (Eq. 8). The same procedure is used on both continents. US 2018 was excluded from training because Sentinel-2 imagery was insufficient (Section 2.1.2). The paper does not say how gaps at the edges (March, October) are handled, since a two-sided interpolation is undefined there (not verified). Normalization is not relevant for RF.

**(3) Does missingness differ between regions?** Image counts per area are reported in Table 1. Training states have about 5,500 to 7,000 images per year. Mapping countries in 2018 have 6,820 (Romania) to 16,093 (Spain). The counts are not normalised by area, and there are no per-pixel valid-observation statistics. QA60 is a coarse cloud mask, so residual clouds are possible in cloudier target countries. That is not analysed.

**(4) Verdict: unlikely.** The "failure" headline is a comparison of different source states applied to the same target composites, so target-side missingness is held fixed across the models being compared. The authors explain the spread with temperature-profile similarity, which is winter versus spring wheat phenology (PCC > 0.6 in five of six countries). That mechanism is independent of gaps. Gaps are filled by in-distribution interpolation identically everywhere. *Data check:* per state and country, the fraction of pixels per month that required interpolation (and edge-month handling). Then test whether the fraction differs between South Dakota or Minnesota and Kansas. Only a source-side difference could matter here.

## 4. Reuss et al., EuroCropsML (Sci. Data 2025, doi 10.1038/s41597-025-04952-7; benchmark ISPRS Open J. 2026, 19:100117)

**(1) Claimed transfer drop and availability.** This is not a zero-shot transfer test. A Transformer is pre-trained on Latvia, or on Latvia plus Portugal, then fine-tuned on k = 1 to 500 Estonian parcels per class. It is scored on a random 20% of Estonian parcels. The "transfer failure" is that adding Portugal lowers overall accuracy for every algorithm. For example, from-scratch training beats all LV+PT methods at 1 to 20 shots, and plain transfer learning from LV is below scratch at 500 shots (0.575 versus 0.632) (briefer, Tables 4 and 6). Finding 5 says geographic transfer is hard for all supervised methods. Code: https://github.com/dida-do/eurocropsml (dataset), https://github.com/jsreu/EurocropsML-Meta-Learning and https://github.com/jsreu/EuroCropsML-SSL. Data are on Zenodo (doi 10.5281/zenodo.10629609).

**(2) Missing-data handling.** Sentinel-2 L1C TOA is used, with a per-date parcel median, an irregular sequence of 1 to 216 steps for 2021, and no grid and no interpolation. Cloud removal is a heuristic on B4 reflectance: probability 0 below 0.07, 1 above 0.25, linear in between, and observations above 0.5 are dropped (dataset paper Section "Cloud removal"; `eurocropsml/dataset/preprocess.py`, `find_clouds` and `_filter_clouds`, lines 122-162, applied in `_save_row` at lines 211-212, commit 8988f72). Padding rows of −999 are removed (lines 113-119). In the model, dates enter through the positional encoding. In `eurocropsmeta/models/transformer.py` lines 64-69 (commit 1b87181), a padding mask is passed as `src_key_padding_mask`. Data are scaled to (0,1] with an epsilon so that the 0 pad cannot collide with real values (audit, `dataset.py` lines 165-174). I did not verify whether output pooling excludes pads.

**(3) Does missingness differ between regions?** Almost certainly, in observation density. Portugal is much less cloudy than the Baltics, so cloud filtering should leave longer sequences there. No per-country sequence-length or cloud-removal statistics are reported in either paper (not found). A B4 brightness threshold also misses cloud shadows and thin haze, and those residuals could differ by climate.

**(4) Verdict: unlikely.** Nothing is filled. Missing dates are simply absent, and padding is masked, so the constant-into-an-always-observed-step mechanism cannot occur. The authors' explanation for the LV+PT loss (crop mix, climate, phenology, and the fact that Estonia-only classes gain nothing) is well supported by their seen-versus-unseen class analysis. A density shift between Portugal and the Baltics could still play a minor role, but it would act through sequence length and not through an out-of-distribution value. *Data check:* from the npz files, plot per-country histograms of sequence length and day-of-year coverage, and the share of residual bright or dark outliers. Then test fine-tuned accuracy as a function of Estonian sequence length.

## 5. Kerner et al. 2024 (Sci. Data 11:486, PMC11087537) and Kerner et al. 2020 (arXiv 2006.16866)

**(1) Claimed transfer drop and availability.** Kerner et al. 2024, "How accurate are existing land cover maps for agriculture in Sub-Saharan Africa?", evaluates 11 existing global or regional land-cover and cropland products against 3,386 reference points in Kenya, Malawi, Mali, Rwanda, Tanzania, Togo, Uganda and Zambia. Mean F1 across maps ranges from 0.21 ± 0.22 (Mali) to 0.71 ± 0.16 (Rwanda), and all maps agree on cropland in under 0.5% of pixels. No model is trained or transferred. The products' own training regions and pipelines are outside the study. Code: https://github.com/nasaharvest/crop-mask/blob/master/src/compare_covermaps.py. Reference data are on Zenodo and GEE.

Kerner et al. 2020, "Rapid Response Crop Maps in Data Sparse Regions", is the Togo cropland LSTM. It is trained on hand-labelled Togo points plus the global crowdsourced See (2017) dataset, and tested on 306 in-country consensus points (Table 1, best F1 0.74 to 0.76). There is no cross-region test, so it reports no transfer failure. Code: https://github.com/nasaharvest/togo-crop-mask.

**(2) Missing-data handling.** For 2024, the products' pipelines are not analysed. The paper mentions cloud cover only as a factor cited by prior work (Introduction), along with Esri's least-cloudy-scene selection (Methods).

For 2020, Section 2.2 uses 12 30-day periods, each the least-cloudy pixel per Schmitt et al. (GEE). In the code (commit 61da135), `BaseEngineer.maxed_nan_to_num` (`src/engineer/base.py` lines 213-221) applies `np.nan_to_num(nan=0)` to raw values. Training instances with more than 30% NaN are dropped (`max_nan_ratio=0.3`, base.py line 275; `src/engineer/togo.py` lines 77-78), and the normalizing statistics are then computed on the zero-filled arrays (togo.py lines 80-81). At inference, `tif_to_np` (`src/models/utils.py` lines 52-55) zero-fills raw NaNs with no NaN-ratio cap and then applies the training mean and std. This is structurally our failure pattern: a raw constant written before a training-fitted normalizer, with screening in training but not at inference.

**(3) Does missingness differ between regions?** For 2024, there is no source/target design to test. For 2020, there is a single region, and NaNs only arise when a 30-day window has no acquisition at all, because the least-cloudy-pixel mosaic picks something whenever an image exists.

**(4) Verdict: unlikely (2024 not assessable; 2020 no transfer claim).** The 2024 paper's map errors cannot be attributed to missingness from what is reported. For 2020, the zero-fill code path matches ours, but the paper reports no transfer failure and the quality mosaic makes NaN timesteps rare. It is worth one sentence as evidence that the pattern exists in operational code. *Data check (2020):* fraction of NaN entries per timestep in the Togo inference tiles versus the training instances. Also check whether any timestep is almost never NaN in training but NaN for a block of inference pixels.

## 6. Valinho (António) et al. 2024, GoodIT '24, ACM, doi 10.1145/3677525.3678696

The full text was not accessible. thehive.icipe.org PDF and repository.rsif-paset.org were blocked (Cloudflare challenge or connection refused), and ACM DL and ResearchGate returned 403. What follows comes from the abstract (thehive.icipe.org record) and search-engine snippets of the full text. All of it is not verified against the paper.

**(1) Claimed transfer drop and availability.** Baraouéli (Mali) and Karamoja (Uganda), Sentinel-2, with MLP, RF and SVM. Local accuracy is high, but performance "declined when models were transferred". Restricting to maize and sorghum improves transfer, though it stays below local accuracy. I could not obtain the numbers. Data are from Source Cooperative (exact dataset names not verified), and the funding note also names "Northern Uganda data". No code was found.

**(2) Missing-data handling.** A search snippet says Sentinel-2 L1C TOA scenes with less than 20% cloud cover were collected from May to September, for 2017 (Karamoja) and 2019 (Baraouéli). Pixel-level cloud masking, compositing, the time grid, gap filling and normalization are not verified.

**(3) Does missingness differ between regions?** Plausibly. The two regions differ in climate (Sahelian versus East African semi-arid), and the window falls in the rainy season. The years also differ, so the number of scenes passing a scene-level 20% filter, and the residual cloud inside them, likely differ. A scene-level filter on L1C leaves cloudy pixels in as raw values unless pixel masking is applied. No per-region observation counts are known to me.

**(4) Verdict: possible.** If the snippet is accurate, cloud contamination may be left in raw TOA values in a cloudy season, with different years and regions. That leaves room for a missingness contribution. But the transfer is also confounded by different years, crop calendars and class sets, and the maize-sorghum restriction helping shows class mismatch explains part of it. Without the full text this cannot go beyond "possible". *Data check:* obtain the full text. Then, per region, count scenes per month passing the filter and the per-pixel s2cloudless cloud fraction at the training samples, and confirm how per-date features were aligned between regions with different acquisition dates.

## 7. Chen Shiyang & Liu Jia 2021, Trans. CSAE 37(15):161-168, doi 10.11975/j.issn.1002-6819.2021.15.020

The English and Chinese abstract pages on aeeisp.com were readable. The full text (tcsae.org, the DOI target) refused connection and the HTML full-text links are JavaScript-only, so the full text is not verified.

**(1) Claimed transfer drop and availability.** GF-6 WFV, April to November, Heilongjiang. Models are TempCNN, MSResNet, OmniscaleCNN, LSTM, StarRNN and Transformer, with RF as the baseline. Six classes (rice, maize, soybean, water, urban, rest). Trained in Lindian (OA 93 to 95% for all models) and applied to Hailun. Overall accuracy fell by 7.2 to 41.0%. MSResNet changed least, LSTM and StarRNN dropped by less than 10%, and Transformer and RF "dropped significantly". Per-model transfer numbers are not in the abstract. No code or data release was found.

**(2) Missing-data handling.** The abstract says "image interpolation and global min-max normalization were also applied to fill the empty pixel". The interpolation method, whether it ran before or after normalization, cloud masking, and how the series were aligned are not verified. Lindian had 41 images and Hailun 48. A fixed-input RF (and probably the CNNs) needs equal-length inputs, so some resampling or date matching must have occurred. How it was done is not verified.

**(3) Does missingness differ between regions?** Yes, at least in acquisition pattern. The two sites have different image counts (41 versus 48), and the authors attribute the decline to "differences of land cover classes and temporal composition of the data" (abstract). Per-date cloud statistics are not available to me.

**(4) Verdict: possible.** This is the paper where the authors themselves name the temporal composition of the data as a cause. The pattern of losses also fits a sensitivity to which dates are present: RF and the Transformer, which bind features to specific steps, dropped most, while the recurrent models dropped least. But "temporal composition" here means differing acquisition schedules and possibly interpolated pixels, not necessarily a constant fill. Different class sets are also named. *Data check:* the full text, giving the acquisition dates per site, the interpolation and alignment method, and the fraction of interpolated pixels per date at each site. Then test whether the per-model loss tracks reliance on dates that are interpolated or shifted in Hailun.
