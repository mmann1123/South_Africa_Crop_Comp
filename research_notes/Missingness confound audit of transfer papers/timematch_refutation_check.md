# TimeMatch refutation check: does anything in Nyborg et al. 2022 disprove or weaken our cloud-confound claim?

Paper: Nyborg, Pelletier, Lefèvre, Assent 2022, "TimeMatch: Unsupervised cross-region adaptation by temporal shift estimation", ISPRS JPRS 188:301–313, doi 10.1016/j.isprsjprs.2022.04.018. Read in full: the journal PDF (13 pp., `writeup/literature/TimeMatch: ...pdf`), arXiv 2111.02682 v1 (14 pp.) and v3 (16 pp.), the GitHub README (jnyborg/timematch), and the Zenodo results archive `trained_models_and_results.zip` (record 5636422, 113 MB; per-fold `overall_*.json` and `class_report_*.txt` for every Table 1 experiment). Page numbers below are journal page numbers.

Claim under test (from `experiments/external_audits/timematch/README.md`): the dataset keeps L1C scenes with tile cloudy-pixel percentage <= 80 and has no per-pixel mask; DK1 (32VNH) is the cloudiest tile (28.6 cloudy / 23.4 clear observations per parcel series; 11.7 clear Apr to Sep vs 18.5 FR1, 13.6 FR2, 26.4 AT1); the paper attributes its 43-point source-only collapse to a calendar shift without testing whether cloud differences contribute, so part of the "temporal shift" loss may be cloud/missingness shift.

## Verdict

Nothing in the paper disproves the narrow claim. The paper contains no cloud test of any kind: no clear-only run, no cloud-filtering ablation, no per-tile cloud statistic, no robustness-to-missing-dates experiment. The only cloud sentence is the 80 % tile filter (p. 308). The statement "the paper never tests whether cloud differences contribute" is intact.

The broader inference, that a meaningful part of the 43-point collapse may be cloud shift rather than calendar shift, is **weakened**. Five pieces of the paper's own evidence point that way, and one is close to a direct test. In Fig. 7a, shifting the target dates of a frozen DK1-trained model by +32 days lifts overall accuracy on FR1 from about 0.57 to about 0.85. The target-trained ceiling is 0.97. The cloud content of the inputs is identical in both runs. So for the cloudiest-source pair, a pure time-axis relabel recovers roughly 70 % of the overall-accuracy gap with no retraining. The per-class reports in the Zenodo archive show the collapse has a directional phenology signature: in DK1→FR1, French winter wheat is predicted as winter barley (wheat recall 0.06, barley recall 0.98 at precision 0.12). Cloud contamination would not produce that, and the shift fixes it (wheat F1 0.11 to 0.96).

What survives is a residual claim. After shift correction a sizeable gap remains: macro-F1 24 points on average, and 38.5 points on FR1→DK1. On FR1→DK1, accuracy recovery is only 51 % against 83 % for DK1→FR1. The paper does not explain that residual. The worst-recovered pair is the one whose target is the cloudy Danish tile, and the largest unresolved class there is DK1 spring barley (recall 0.31 after TimeMatch). Spring barley's season (Apr to Jul) falls in DK1's months with the fewest clear observations. This fits a cloud contribution but does not isolate one: spring barley is also rare in the FR1 source (77 test parcels), so the source model has little to learn it from.

Suggested rewording: keep "the paper never tests clouds" and "DK1 is cloudiest". Drop or soften "part of the temporal-shift loss may be cloud shift". Replace it with "temporal alignment recovers most of the overall-accuracy loss but leaves a residual macro-F1 gap that is largest when the cloudy Danish tile is the target; the paper does not test whether cloud differences explain that residual." Also fix the README's "30 to 35 day shift": the paper's estimates are 32, 42, 16, −35 and 4 days (Fig. 4 inset).

## Evidence against our claim (weakening), strongest first

1. **Pure date shift of a frozen source model recovers most of the overall-accuracy gap (Fig. 7a, p. 310; Sec. 5.2).** "In Fig. 7a, we show the change in the overall accuracy of a source-trained model when applied to target data with different temporal shifts for DK1→FR1 ... We observe a significant increase in accuracy by temporally shifting the target data." These values were read off the rendered figure, so they are approximate. Overall accuracy is about 0.57 at shift 0, consistent with the archive's source-only accuracy of 57.4 %. It peaks at about 0.85 at shift +32 (dashed line). The archive's target-trained accuracy is 97.0 % and TimeMatch's is 90.4 %. Cloud contamination is unchanged by this manipulation, so in DK1→FR1 at most about 12 accuracy points (0.97 − 0.85) remain for every non-shift cause together (clouds, spectra, soil, class priors). Caveats: this is overall accuracy, which large classes (meadow, corn, winter wheat) dominate, not macro F1; it covers only one pair and apparently one model; and DK1 is the source there, so cloudy observations are in training, not in the test inputs.

2. **The collapse has a directional phenology signature that a shift repairs (Zenodo archive class reports; Fig. 9 and Sec. 5.5, p. 311).** DK1→FR1 source-only (fold 0): winter_wheat P 0.75 R 0.06 F1 0.11; winter_barley P 0.12 R 0.98; winter_rapeseed R 0.57; corn F1 0.94. TimeMatch: winter_wheat F1 0.96, winter_barley 0.83, rapeseed 0.94. FR1→DK1 source-only: winter_rapeseed R 0.04, winter_barley R 0.08, winter_wheat R 0.36. TimeMatch: rapeseed 0.97, barley 0.98, wheat 0.83. The paper says: "We observe a large class confusion for the source-trained model, in particular between winter barley (blue) and winter wheat (dark pink) ... TimeMatch resolves this issue" (p. 311). Earlier-maturing French wheat looks like Danish barley. That is a timing error, not a noise error, and it is the error that the shift corrects.

3. **Methods that could absorb a cloud-distribution shift but do not model time fail (Table 1, p. 309; Sec. 5.1 and 5.1 last paragraph, p. 310).** MMD, DANN, CDAN+E, ALDA and JUMBOT align source and target feature distributions. A difference in cloud mixture is exactly the kind of marginal shift they target. They lift the average from 39.2 to only 41.5–47.4. FixMatch, which self-trains on target data without a shift, does worse than source-only on 4 of 5 pairs (DK1→FR1 24.2 vs 28.3). Quote: "Existing UDA methods, however, only slightly increase the performance of source-trained models ... By incorporating our ShiftAug, we observe a performance boost across all evaluated methods." Domain-specific batch norm, which re-estimates target input statistics, matters little in the ablation (56.9 vs 57.4, Table 2, p. 310). Removing the source shift costs 5.5 points (51.9).

4. **FR1→FR2 has a large cloud-regime difference but a small collapse.** By our numbers FR1 has 25.0 cloudy observations per series and FR2 has 12.3. Date counts are 62 vs 39 (season clear observations 18.5 vs 13.6). Yet the estimated shift is 4 days and the source-only gap is only 15.4 macro-F1 points (70.3 vs 85.7) and 6.9 accuracy points. Across the five pairs, the gap tracks the shift better than it tracks cloud-count mismatch, with DK1→AT1 as the exception (point 2 in the next list). The paper's own reading: "the source and target regions are the most geographically close, and as result, the temporal shift is also closer to zero" (p. 310).

5. **The shift estimates are internally consistent and track climate (Sec. 5.2, pp. 310–311; Fig. 4 inset).** "the temporal shift ... from the warmer FR1 (mid-west France) to the colder DK1 (Denmark) is estimated as 32 days ... In the other direction ... we estimate a negative temporal shift of −35 days ... the two source-trained models used to estimate the temporal shift in each direction are trained with two completely separate source region, yet their estimated shifts are still roughly inverses." A cloud artefact would not obviously yield near-antisymmetric, climatically ordered shifts. Fig. 1 (p. 302) shows a clean, roughly 30 to 40 day offset in winter-wheat senescence (FR1 NDVI falls around day 165–185, DK1 around day 200–215).

6. **The architecture is partly designed for irregular sampling (Sec. 3.1, p. 303; Sec. 4.1, p. 307; Sec. 4.4, p. 309).** Day-of-year positional encoding "makes it possible to account for the irregular temporal sampling of most satellites". Training randomly sub-samples 30 time steps per example ("We augment the inputs by randomly sub-sampling 30 time steps"). FixMatch's strong augmentation is also random time-step sub-sampling. In-domain macro-F1 is 91.2 (DK1) and 92.2 (FR1) in the archive, so the model copes with each tile's own clouds. This weakens the idea that clouds per se cripple PSE+LTAE. It does not address a *difference* in cloud regime between source and target, because sub-sampling drops dates but never removes cloudy values, and attention learns only the source's cloud pattern.

7. **The paper never claims the whole collapse is temporal (Sec. 3.1, p. 304).** "In this work, we focus on the domain discrepancies caused by temporal shifts ... Although not explicitly addressed in this work, there are other sources of discrepancies that might occur. For example, the local topography or soil conditions could impact ... the spectral values." The abstract says models "perform poorly ... due to the temporal shift of crop phenology between regions" (p. 301), but the body hedges. If we say the paper "attributes the 43-point collapse to a calendar shift", we should cite the abstract and acknowledge this hedge.

## Evidence consistent with our claim (direct only)

1. **No cloud handling beyond the tile filter (Sec. 4.2, p. 308).** "Sentinel-2 Level-1C products in top-of-atmosphere reflectance ... We use all available observations with cloud coverage ⩽80% and coverage ⩾50% between January 2017 and December 2017 ... We leave out the atmospheric bands (1, 9, and 10)." No other mention of clouds, cloud masks, haze, shadow or snow appears in the journal text or any arXiv version. The only "noise" mentions concern pseudo-labels and label noise from parcel borders. Dropping B01/B09/B10 also removes the bands that s2cloudless needs.

2. **Shift correction leaves a large residual, largest with DK1 as target.** Recovery of the target-minus-source gap by TimeMatch (archive, 3-fold means): macro-F1 63 % (DK1→FR1), 41 % (DK1→FR2), 42 % (DK1→AT1), 42 % (FR1→DK1), 18 % (FR1→FR2). Overall accuracy: 83, 54, 50, 51, 14 %. FR1→DK1 keeps 38.5 macro-F1 points and 24.4 accuracy points after TimeMatch. In the reverse direction (DK1→FR1) 17.2 and 6.6 remain. This asymmetry fits "cloudy target is harder". It is confounded by different class sets, class frequencies and upper bounds, though (90.6 vs 74.6 macro-F1).

3. **DK1→AT1: small shift, large gap.** The estimated shift is only 16 days, yet the source-only gap is 43.5 macro-F1 points. That is as large as DK1→FR1 (46.4, 32-day shift) and DK1→FR2 (43.4, 42-day shift). It is also the largest cloud mismatch in the set (season clear observations 11.7 DK1 vs 26.4 AT1). The accuracy gap is only 14.9 points, though, so the loss is concentrated in rare classes.

4. **The unresolved residual in FR1→DK1 sits in the cloudiest season.** After TimeMatch, DK1 spring_barley (2,801 test parcels, the largest DK1 class) has recall 0.31 and F1 0.45. The unknown class has F1 0.50. Every winter crop is above 0.83. Spring barley develops April to July, when DK1 averages 1.8, 1.2, 0.8 and 2.4 clear observations per month. Confound: spring_barley is rare in the FR1 source (77 FR1 test parcels in fold 0).

5. **Fig. 1 (p. 302) shows cloud-like single-date NDVI drops**, e.g. FR1 spring barley around day 160 and many late-season drops to 0.25–0.30 in FR1 after day 230. The caption does not say whether the curves are single parcels or class means. They are illustrative only, and DK1's growing-season curves look smoother than FR1's, so this does not single out Denmark.

Extra source-only results from the archive that the paper does not report (macro-F1 / OA): FR1→AT1 48.6 / 81.9 (no target-trained run with FR1 classes); in-domain DK1→DK1 91.2 / 94.7 and FR1→FR1 92.2 / 96.9. Asymmetry check (item 3 of the brief): DK1→FR1 and FR1→DK1 collapse to similar levels (28.3 vs 24.9). A symmetric collapse is what both a shift and a cloud-regime mismatch predict, so source-only numbers alone do not discriminate. The post-correction asymmetry (residual 17 vs 38.5 macro-F1; 6.6 vs 24.4 OA) is the only pair-level pattern pointing at the cloudy target. Its confounds are given in point 2 of the previous list.

ShiftAug alone (shift invariance by ±60-day random shifts, Sec. 4.3, p. 309) recovers 23 % of the average macro-F1 gap (39.2→49.0). TimeMatch recovers 44 %. On macro-F1, explicit shift handling therefore closes less than half the gap. On overall accuracy it closes more (Fig. 7a and the OA column). The unexplained remainder lies mostly in rare classes.

## Items 1–6 of the brief, briefly

1. Cloud/noise controls: none. No clear-only dates, no cloud-filter ablation, no missing-date robustness test. The shift estimator (Eq. 7, Algorithm 1) runs on target data as shipped, with no subset selection. The only cloud filter is the 80 % tile filter (p. 308).
2. Does a pure shift restore accuracy? For DK1→FR1 overall accuracy, mostly yes (Fig. 7a, about 0.57→0.85 of 0.97). For macro-F1 across pairs, ShiftAug recovers 23 % and TimeMatch 44 % of the gap. No pure-shift macro-F1 number is reported.
3. Pair-level: see the table. FR1→FR2 (no Denmark, small shift, large cloud-count mismatch) barely collapses. DK1→AT1 (small shift, large clear-count mismatch) collapses fully. FR1→DK1 is worst before and after correction.
4. Mean time series: only Fig. 1 (four crops, NDVI, DK1 vs FR1). It shows a clear phenological offset plus some cloud-like single-date drops, mainly in FR1. There are no per-tile class-mean figures for AT1 or FR2.
5. Own discussion of clouds/limitations: clouds are not discussed. Limitations named are the gap to supervised performance, a call for "stronger temporal alignments, e.g. class-wise alignments or time warping" (p. 312), and unaddressed spectral/soil discrepancies (p. 304).
6. Robustness to irregular/cloudy dates: day-of-year encoding and random 30-step sub-sampling give robustness to irregular sampling and missing dates. Cloudy values are never removed, and nothing makes the model robust to a change in cloud regime. In-domain F1 above 91 shows clouds are not fatal within a tile.
