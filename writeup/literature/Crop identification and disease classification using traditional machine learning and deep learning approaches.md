# Crop identification and disease classification using traditional machine learning and deep learning approaches

**Citation:** Aravind Krishnaswamy Rangarajan, Raja Purushothaman, Maheswari Prabhakar, and Cezary Szczepański (2023). *Journal of Engineering Research*, 11(1B), 228-252. DOI: 10.36909/jer.11941

## Objectives

In a multicropping field, the paper builds a single ground-based image system that performs three nested tasks: (1) identify the crop (eggplant vs tomato) from a whole-plant top-view photo, (2) detect health status (healthy vs unhealthy eggplant), and (3) classify the disease (Cercospora leaf spot vs two-spotted spider-mite infestation) on eggplant. The comparison is between four shallow machine-learning algorithms (linear discriminant analysis, Naive Bayes, SVM, neural network) using hand-engineered texture descriptors versus a deep-learning model (`AlexNet`) using raw images. This is a proximal-sensing / plant-pathology study, not a satellite crop-type-mapping study.

## Methods

Images were captured in-field at mid-day with a 16-MP mobile-phone camera (Xiaomi Redmi Note 3) over a Thanjavur, India field. The created dataset is small (eggplant 135, tomato 56; healthy 39 / unhealthy 96; spider-mite 57 / Cercospora 39) and was expanded by augmentation (rotation, translation, intensity modification) to 810/448/234/576/342/237. Plants were segmented from the soil background (green-channel thresholding to RGB to gray-scale). For crop identification, GLCM textural descriptors (contrast, correlation, homogeneity, energy) were extracted from the gray-scale image; for disease classification, the RGB was converted to HSV then to gray-scale before the same four GLCM descriptors; for health status, six histogram-based descriptors (average gray level, average contrast, smoothness, third moment, uniformity, entropy) were used. Shallow classifiers consumed these descriptors with class-prior weighting; `AlexNet` (pretrained, transfer-learned) consumed raw images.

**Evaluation protocol:** 10-fold cross-validation on the (augmented) image dataset — the data are split into 10 subsets, each serving once as test. This is image-level k-fold within a single field/session, with no spatially disjoint holdout, no cross-field or cross-site test, and no cross-season test. The critical leakage concern is that **augmentation appears to precede the train/test split**: 191 original images were expanded ~6x to >2,000, and rotated/translated/intensity-shifted copies of the *same physical plant* can land in both train and test folds, which would substantially inflate accuracy (the reported 100% AlexNet crop-identification accuracy is consistent with this). There is no spectral or temporal time-series dimension at all — these are single RGB snapshots. The paper reports overall accuracy per task; it does not report macro-F1, per-class F1, or Kappa, so minority-class behavior is not quantified.

## Key Findings

Crop identification (eggplant vs tomato) was easy for every method: shallow GLCM-based accuracies were 97.61% (LDA), 95.62% (Naive Bayes), 98.01% (SVM), 98.94% (NN), and AlexNet reached 100%. Disease classification was harder: shallow accuracies 86.09% / 76.52% / 86.96% / 86.04%, AlexNet 81.51%. Health-status detection by AlexNet was 89.36%. A simpler 6-histogram-descriptor variant did markedly worse (66.67% / 37.04% / 50% / 72.9%), indicating descriptor choice dominates shallow performance. Across all three tasks AlexNet had the best *average* accuracy (90.29%), but it did not win every individual task — shallow texture methods beat it on disease classification. The authors frame the work as a preliminary pilot for an integrated crop-plus-disease diagnosis system in a multicropping garden/field.

## Relevance to Our Crop-Classification Study

This paper is a weak fit for our problem and should be cited mainly as a methodological contrast, not as evidence on satellite crop mapping. Three points are transferable. (1) It reiterates the deep-vs-classical theme at the heart of our manuscript — and notably finds that hand-engineered texture features fed to shallow classifiers *beat* the deep net (`AlexNet`) on the harder disease task, while the deep net only edges ahead on the trivially separable crop-ID task. That pattern (engineered-feature classical models competitive-to-superior when the task is hard or data are scarce) rhymes with our finding that sparse, feature-selecting models out-transfer dense deep nets. (2) The small-sample regime (135/56 originals) and reliance on augmentation underline how data scarcity favors lower-capacity, regularized models — relevant to our field-reduction ablation. (3) The GLCM texture-descriptor pipeline is a reminder that engineered features remain competitive. Beyond that, the differences are large: ground-based RGB single snapshots (no Sentinel-2, no multi-temporal sequence, no field-level satellite aggregation, no spatial transfer), a 2-4 class proximal task, and a different objective (disease detection). Our band set, temporal features, and FID-level aggregation have no analogue here.

## Evaluation Caveats

The most serious caveat is augmentation-before-split leakage: rotated/translated copies of the same plant almost certainly cross fold boundaries, which inflates accuracy and likely explains the 100% AlexNet crop-ID result — this is in-region k-fold with within-sample leakage, not a generalization test. There is no spatially disjoint holdout, no cross-field/cross-site/cross-season validation, so nothing here speaks to transfer. Only overall accuracy is reported (no macro-F1, per-class F1, or Kappa), so the class imbalance (e.g., 39 healthy vs 96 unhealthy; 39 Cercospora vs 57 spider-mite) is unaddressed and minority-class recall is invisible. The dataset is tiny, single-site, single-session, and the conclusions are explicitly preliminary. Sensor design is proximal RGB only (no multispectral, no SAR, no temporal). Cite this for the qualitative "engineered-feature classical models stay competitive with deep nets, especially on hard/scarce tasks" observation, not for any accuracy figure as a transfer benchmark.
