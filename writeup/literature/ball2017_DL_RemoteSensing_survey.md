This is a survey/theory paper, so it is briefed under rule 7: no evaluation-protocol critique applies; the focus is on methodological and conceptual implications — inductive bias, transfer learning, interpretability, and data scarcity in remote-sensing deep learning.

## Objectives

The survey reviews 207 remote-sensing application papers and 57 deep-learning survey papers to give the remote-sensing (RS) community a comprehensive map of state-of-the-art deep learning (DL). Beyond cataloguing applications, the authors' primary contribution is a structured enumeration of nine unsolved challenges / open questions in DL-for-RS: (i) inadequate (small) datasets, (ii) human-understandable / interpretable solutions for physical phenomena, (iii) Big Data, (iv) non-traditional heterogeneous data sources, (v) DL architectures for spectral/spatial/temporal data, (vi) transfer learning, (vii) improved theoretical understanding of DL, (viii) high barriers to entry, and (ix) training and optimizing DL. They also contrast "deep" vs. "shallow" learning and provide a tool survey.

## Methods

This is a narrative literature survey and taxonomy, not an empirical study. The authors organize DL architectures (autoencoders/`AE`, `CNN`, deep belief networks/`DBN`, recurrent nets/`RNN`/`LSTM`, deconvolutional nets) and cluster RS applications into roughly twelve areas (classification, multi-sensor fusion, rotation-invariant detection, `SAR`, ocean/atmosphere, 3D, spectral-spatial `HSI`, multi-temporal analysis, change detection, etc.). For each open question they synthesize what prior work has and has not solved. The conceptual spine relevant to us is their deep-vs-shallow contrast: shallow learners (`SVM`, Gaussian mixtures, HMMs, conditional random fields, logistic regression) compute one-to-two-layer functions of (often hand-designed) features; DL stacks many nonlinear layers to learn hierarchical features directly from data. They cite expressivity theory (Cybenko's universal approximation; Telgarsky and Liang showing deep nets need exponentially fewer neurons than shallow ones to represent highly oscillatory functions) as the formal case for depth.

## Key Findings

For our manuscript the load-bearing themes are the open questions, not any benchmark number. (1) **Data scarcity is the dominant RS challenge.** The authors stress that, ironically, RS often *lacks* sufficiently varied training data despite massive raw volume — seasonal/illumination/object variety is frequently missing — so DL models are starved exactly where they need data, making them prone to overfitting. (2) **Transfer fails under shift.** They state plainly that "typically in remote sensing, when changing sensors or changing to a different part of a large image or other imagery collected at different times, the transfer fails," and that transfer must be made robust to imagery collected at different times and under different atmospheric conditions — these are open problems. (3) **Interpretability / black-box concern.** RS end-users (scientists, biologists) need to understand *how reliable* a decision is and how the model arrived at it; DL is criticized as a black box relative to a linear model whose parameters are well understood. (4) **Regularization and generalization.** The training section catalogues the regularizers DL needs to generalize (`L1`/`L2` penalties, sparsity-enforcing regularizers, dropout, early stopping, batch normalization, data augmentation) and cites Sokolić et al. that a network generalizes well only when the spectral norm of its Jacobian near the training samples is bounded — i.e., uncontrolled capacity is the failure mode. (5) DL's strength is hierarchical spatial/spectral feature learning; multi-temporal and change-detection analysis are noted as especially hard because change occurs on multiple timescales and ground-truth is expensive.

## Relevance to Our Crop-Classification Study

This survey provides the conceptual scaffolding and prior-art warning for our central finding. The authors independently articulate, in 2017, the exact mechanisms our results demonstrate: that RS DL models overfit under data scarcity, and that **transfer fails when moving to a different part of a large image or to imagery collected at a different time** — which is precisely the spatially disjoint holdout tile (`34S_20E_259N`) on which our dense temporal/patch nets collapse. Our study can be positioned as an empirical, controlled instance of their "transfer learning" and "inadequate datasets" open questions, with a concrete protocol (field-wise k-fold in-region vs. spatially disjoint holdout) that quantifies the misranking they warn about qualitatively. Their deep-vs-shallow framing and their emphasis on regularization/Jacobian-bounded generalization support our inductive-bias argument: shallow, sparse, regularized learners (gradient-boosted trees, and sparse-selection `TabNet`) carry stronger generalization guarantees under shift than high-capacity dense nets. Their interpretability concern reinforces our motivation for preferring feature-selecting models whose decisions are auditable by agronomic end-users. The survey also legitimizes hybrid and ensemble strategies (deep features + shallow classifiers, multi-DL fusion) that align with our ensemble-vote and field-level-aggregation remedies. Notably, the survey is optical/`SAR`-agnostic but flags `SAR` noise and `HSI` dimensionality challenges; our optical-only Sentinel-2 design (`B2`, `B6`, `B11`, `B12`, `EVI`, hue, no `SAR`) sidesteps the `SAR` issues while squarely inhabiting their multi-temporal-analysis hard case.

## Evaluation Caveats

As a 2017 survey it predates the temporal-attention architectures (e.g., L-TAE, TempCNN) central to our comparison, so its multi-temporal discussion is general rather than architecture-specific. It reports no quantitative transfer experiment — its transfer-failure claims are synthesized from cited work, not measured, so it motivates but does not test our hypothesis. The survey treats interpretability and generalization as open *problems* without prescribing the field-wise-disjoint vs. spatially-disjoint protocol distinction our study makes operational; in that sense its silence on a standardized OOS evaluation protocol is itself a finding — the misranking risk we document was a known-but-unquantified gap. It is breadth-over-depth: any single application area (crop classification, multi-temporal optical) is covered briefly, and class-imbalance / balanced-metric concerns (our macro-F1 emphasis) are not foregrounded. Finally, the local filename attributes the work to "Zhu 2017," which is incorrect; bibliographic entries should use Ball, Anderson & Chan (2017) to avoid a citation error.
