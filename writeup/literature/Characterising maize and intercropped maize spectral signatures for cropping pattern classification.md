# Characterising maize and intercropped maize spectral signatures for cropping pattern classification

**Citation:** Mbali Mahlayeye, Roshanak Darvishzadeh, and Andrew Nelson (2024). *International Journal of Applied Earth Observation and Geoinformation*, Vol. 128, Article 103699. DOI: 10.1016/j.jag.2024.103699

## Objectives

The study analyzes how multi-temporal Sentinel-2 spectral signatures of single-cropped maize versus intercropped maize (`imaize`) differ across crop growth phases, in smallholder rainfed fields of Busia County, western Kenya. The goal is to identify the critical phenological window, the most discriminating spectral bands and vegetation indices (VIs), and to classify the two cropping patterns with a Random Forest (`RF`) classifier. The motivating problem is that intercropped fields are routinely misclassified because their spectral response converges on that of single-cropped fields; the authors ask whether a phenology-aligned temporal approach can separate them.

## Methods

The data are field-level: average reflectance and standard deviation were extracted per field from delineated field boundaries (50 maize fields, 37 `imaize` fields; 87 fields total, verified by farmer interviews of 240 farmers and on-site GPS). Five Sentinel-2 surface-reflectance (Level 2A) acquisitions (March-August 2019) were aligned to four maize growth phases (emergence-seedling, vegetative, flowering-yield formation, ripening) plus a post-harvest period. Ten 10-m and 20-m bands were used (B1, B9, B10 excluded; 20-m bands resampled to 10 m); eleven VIs were computed (`NDVI`, `NDWI`, `NDRE2`, `NDRE`, `GNDVI`, `SIPI`, `EVI`, `RECl`, `MTCI`, `REI`, `NDRE3`), most built on red-edge and near-infrared bands. The Mann-Whitney *U* test (non-parametric) flagged bands/phases with significant maize-vs-`imaize` separation. Five RF feature scenarios were tested per phase (all bands; significant bands; VIs; all bands + VIs; significant bands + VIs), `ntree`=50, `max_depth`=4, `mtry`=sqrt, scikit-learn.

**Evaluation protocol:** A single-scene, in-region random split — 65% train / 35% validation on a pooled set of 87 fields from one study area, with no spatially disjoint holdout, no cross-year test, and no k-fold cross-validation reported. Splitting is field-level (each field contributes one averaged feature vector, so there is no within-field pixel leakage), but train and validation fields are interspersed across the same ~950 km² scene and the same 2019 season. This sits at the weak end of the generalization spectrum: it measures within-scene, within-year separability of two cropping patterns, not transfer. Reported user/producer accuracy, overall accuracy, Kappa, and per-class F1 (a balanced metric — good), but on a single 35% validation slice with no resampling, so the metrics carry high variance and no spatial-transfer signal.

## Key Findings

The two cropping patterns are spectrally separable only during the vegetative phase (all bands significant, `p`<0.05) and the flowering-yield phase (most bands significant except `RE740`, `SWIR1614`, `SWIR2202`). At emergence-seedling, ripening, and post-harvest the *U* test found no significant differences — the signatures collapse into a soil/mixed-vegetation response. RF classification at the vegetative phase reached 100% user/producer accuracy, Kappa 1.0, F1 1.0 across all five feature scenarios (a perfect-separation result on a tiny validation set, almost certainly an artifact of small `n` and complete vegetative-phase separation). At the flowering-yield phase, all-bands gave the best result (overall accuracy 86%, Kappa 0.71, maize F1 0.89 / `imaize` F1 0.82); significant bands gave OA 79%; VIs alone gave only OA 66% (Kappa 0.29). Maize consistently outperformed `imaize` in user accuracy, attributed to maize-field weeds (a confounding secondary vegetation) and to the smaller `imaize` sample (`n`=37 vs 50). Red-edge and NIR bands drove the separation; full original bands beat engineered VIs.

## Relevance to Our Crop-Classification Study

This paper directly supports two of our design choices. First, it confirms that field-level aggregation (one averaged feature vector per FID) is a viable, leakage-controlled granularity — exactly our final per-FID prediction strategy — and that operating at field level "enhances understanding of the factors contributing to misclassification." Second, it reinforces our band selection: red-edge and NIR bands carry the discriminative signal, and full spectral bands outperformed derived VIs, echoing our reliance on raw bands (`B2`, `B6`, `B11`, `B12`, `EVI`, `hue`) rather than an index zoo. The phenological-timing finding (only mid-season vegetative/flowering windows separate classes; early and late phases are spectrally degenerate) parallels our exclusion of months `05` and `06` for data-quality reasons and supports the general principle that crop separability is phase-dependent. The weed-as-confounder observation is a useful caveat for our lucerne/medics-dominated classes, where management-driven secondary vegetation could similarly blur signatures. Note that this is a Western Cape-adjacent African smallholder context but a fundamentally different crop problem (maize intercrop detection, not our nine-class menu).

## Evaluation Caveats

The protocol cannot speak to spatial transfer — our central concern — because there is no disjoint holdout tile, no cross-year test, and a single un-resampled 35% validation split. The 100% vegetative-phase accuracy is a small-sample, perfect-separation artifact, not a robustness signal, and should not be read as evidence of strong generalization. Class imbalance (50 vs 37 fields) is acknowledged and is reflected in the lower `imaize` recall; the authors do report a balanced metric (per-class F1 and Kappa), which is good practice, but the tiny test set makes those numbers unstable. The study measures within-region separability only; it is silent on cross-region robustness, cross-year stability, compute cost, and how the result would degrade on an out-of-sample tile. It is optical-only (no SAR), uses engineered time-series alignment to a crop calendar rather than raw temporal sequences fed to a network, and uses a classic tree ensemble — all consistent with the sparse, feature-selecting models that transfer best in our study, but the paper itself provides no transfer evidence.
