# Phenological normalization can improve in-season classification of maize and soybean: A case study in the central US Corn Belt

**Citation:** Kerner, H. R., Sahajpal, R., Pai, D. B., Skakun, S., Puricelli, E., Hosseini, M., Meyer, S., & Becker-Reshef, I. (2022). *Science of Remote Sensing*, 6, 100059. DOI: 10.1016/j.srs.2022.100059 (verified against Crossref: title and venue match).

## Objectives

The paper targets in-season crop type maps (corn, soybean, other) for the US Corn Belt that hold up in years whose planting calendar differs from the training years. The motivating case is 2019, when flooding delayed planting by one to two months. The authors propose "phenology normalization": for each pixel, the time series is re-indexed from calendar dates to three land surface phenology stages (greenup, peak, senescence), and a CNN-LSTM classifies the reflectance at those stages. Three research questions are posed: how to geographically partition training data, how to avoid accuracy loss in a domain-shifted year, and which method works best at early, mid and late season. This is the journal version of the 2020 KDD workshop paper on growth-stage normalization (arXiv 2009.10189).

## Methods

**Data.** Harmonized Landsat and Sentinel-2 (HLS, release 1.4, 30 m, 42 MGRS tiles covering Iowa, Illinois, most of Indiana) plus Sentinel-1 RTC VV and VH from AWS, 2017 to 2020. Bands are B02 to B07 equivalents, NDWI and LSWI, and the two SAR channels (10 channels per timestep). Labels are the USDA Cropland Data Layer (CDL) collapsed to corn, soybean, other, with a 3 x 3 homogeneity filter applied to training samples only. A second test set is 3973 Intelinair field-centre points in Illinois and Indiana for 2020 (62% corn, 33% soybean, 5% other).

**Gap handling (p. 4).** Clouds and shadows are masked with HLS QA bits 0 to 3. Each pixel's HLS series is resampled to 5-day steps and "linear interpolation to fill gaps resulting from clouds or other missing data sources" is applied before greenup, peak and senescence dates are found from NDVI. Sentinel-1 values are taken from the nearest observation in time. The authors note artifacts from undetected cloud shadow and that they chose linear interpolation over Savitzky-Golay for speed. No zero fill or NaN-to-zero step is described.

**Model.** A 2D CNN on 5 x 5 patches and an LSTM on the three-step pixel series, merged by a dense layer, with an extra scalar delta (days from greenup to senescence). Early season uses the greenup step only, mid season greenup and peak, late season all three. A random forest on the same normalized inputs is the only baseline. Training is 100,000 class-balanced points per model, early stopping, and a sieve despeckle filter on all maps. A "regional" model per tile (that tile plus neighbours) is used for the main results after comparing local, universal, cluster and ensemble partitions on Iowa 2019.

**Evaluation protocol.** Cross-year transfer within the same tiles, with random pixel sampling inside each tile. Iowa: train 2017 and 2018, validate 2018, test 2019. Illinois: train 2017 to 2019, validate 2019, test 2020. The test year is the full tile footprint of the same geography, so the setting is temporal transfer, not a spatially disjoint holdout, and the regional model trains on pixels from the test tile itself in earlier years. Reported metrics are overall accuracy and per-class precision, recall and F1 against CDL, plus the Intelinair check. There is no fixed-calendar (DOY) baseline in this paper, so it does not show how much phenology normalization helps versus raw dates; the claim of improved generalization rests on the method's own two test years (Section 6.2 acknowledges only that the metrics are similar across years).

## Key Findings

Late-season overall accuracy against CDL is 0.81 (Iowa 2019, CNN-LSTM-delta) and 0.85 (Illinois 2020). Mid season is 0.80 and 0.84 (CNN), early season 0.71 and 0.72 (CNN). The random forest is lower, notably early and mid season (Iowa 2019 early 0.65, mid 0.73; Illinois 2020 mid 0.73). On Intelinair, overall accuracy is 0.71 early, 0.89 mid, 0.89 late (CDL 2020 itself scores 0.89). The "other" class is weak against Intelinair (F1 0.34 to 0.58). Among tile partitions, the regional model has the best single-model mean accuracy (75.8%, Table 1), and the ensemble adds about 0.4 points. Per-tile accuracy ranges widely (Iowa late season roughly 64% to 90%).

## Relevance to Our Crop-Classification Study

This is a cross-year, same-region comparator in a three-class, two-crop setting, so its scores are not comparable to our five-crop, spatially disjoint holdout. It supports our point that calendar shift is a first-order domain shift, and that a temporal-structure fix (re-indexing to phenology) transfers better than dense models on raw dates, though it never tests that against a raw-date baseline. Gap handling is linear interpolation applied uniformly, which makes the input distribution depend on local cloud rate; this matters for our missingness concern because no per-year valid-observation count is given. Its architecture (CNN plus LSTM on patches) is the kind of dense temporal/patch model our holdout shows overfitting, and it is evaluated only in-tile.

## Evaluation Caveats

Labels are CDL for both training and evaluation, so label noise is shared between them. Pixels are sampled randomly within tiles, and test-year pixels come from the same fields as training years, so spatial and field leakage across years is possible. The validation year is a training-era year. Accuracy is the headline metric; the minority "other" class is poor on Intelinair and macro-F1 or Kappa is not reported. Post-hoc sieve despeckling is applied before scoring. Missingness is not measured: no cloud fraction, valid count or interpolated share per year or tile is reported, and the discussion (Section 6.4) names undetected clouds and phenology-detection noise as error sources without testing them. Cost is discussed only qualitatively (number of models per partition scheme).
