# Land Cover Classification of Remote Sensing Images Based on Hierarchical Convolutional Recurrent Neural Network

**Citation:** Fan, X.; Chen, L.; Xu, X.; Yan, C.; Fan, J.; Li, X. (2023). Land Cover Classification of Remote Sensing Images Based on Hierarchical Convolutional Recurrent Neural Network. *Forests*, 14(9), 1881. DOI: 10.3390/f14091881

## Objectives

Propose a hierarchical convolutional recurrent neural network (**HCRNN**) for pixel-level land-cover/crop classification of multispectral Sentinel-2 imagery, and apply it to map forest, sugarcane, and rice (plus buildup, water, bareland, "otherland") in Laibin City, Guangxi, China for 2017/2019/2021 land-use-change analysis. The architecture fuses a 2D-CNN (for spectral-spatial feature extraction across the 13 Sentinel-2 bands reshaped into a pseudo-image) with four parallel GRU-based RNN branches, extracting CNN features at four hierarchical levels and feeding each into a corresponding recurrent branch before fusing for an MLP-head classification. The stated aim is to beat single-RNN and single-CNN baselines on per-pixel accuracy.

## Methods

Each Sentinel-2 pixel's 13 bands are linearly projected (fully-connected layer) to a 256-vector, reshaped to an 8x8x4 "3D multispectral feature matrix", and passed through a four-layer 2D-CNN (32/64/128/256 kernels). Global average pooling adapts each conv level to a common size; the four levels feed four two-layer GRU stacks; outputs are summed (feature-addition fusion), ReLU-activated, and classified by an MLP head. Loss is cross-entropy; training uses batch 32, 300 iterations, lr-decay 0.9, on an RTX 3060.

Comparison baselines: SVM (RBF), KNN (k=3), RF (100 trees), 1D-CNN, ViT, SpectralFormer, and a two-layer-GRU RNN. Evaluation metrics: overall accuracy (OA), average accuracy (AA), Kappa, plus per-class accuracy and precision. Generalizability is probed on three public hyperspectral benchmarks (Houston, Indian Pines, Pavia University) in addition to the Laibin Sentinel-2 data.

**Evaluation protocol.** This is the load-bearing weakness. The Laibin "sample bank" comprises 24,818 labelled ROI points; for each class, **10% of points are randomly selected for training and the remaining 90% for testing** (a 1:9 random split applied year-by-year). There is NO field-, parcel-, or polygon-disjoint splitting and NO spatial separation between train and test samples. On the leakage spectrum this sits at the **pooled/random split over individual pixels within a single scene** — the most leakage-prone end. Worse for crops: sugarcane and rice samples were deliberately drawn from contiguous planting areas >100 m^2, so randomly splitting points from those same contiguous patches places spatially adjacent, spectrally near-identical pixels in both train and test. The three hyperspectral benchmarks use their standard fixed train/test splits, which for Indian Pines/Pavia are likewise spatially interleaved (a well-known leakage issue in that literature). No spatially disjoint holdout, no cross-year transfer test (the 2017/2019/2021 models are each trained and tested within their own year), and no FID-style blocking are used anywhere. This is a textbook case of in-region random-pixel validation reported as if it measured classification skill.

## Key Findings

- On Laibin 2017, HCRNN reports OA 97.62%, AA 94.68%, Kappa 0.9681 — best of all eight methods, edging SpectralFormer (OA 96.55%) and the plain RNN (95.84%).
- Per-class, HCRNN improves over the single RNN most on the harder/rarer classes: sugarcane +4.65%, rice +9.9%, otherland +4.7%.
- 1D-CNN is consistently worst on the multispectral data (OA 92.79%), attributed to its inability to use spatial structure.
- Classical ML (SVM/KNN/RF) clusters in the mid-90s OA, competitive with but below the DL methods; SVM nonetheless wins some individual classes (e.g., 100% on bareland).
- On hyperspectral benchmarks HCRNN again posts the top OA/AA/Kappa on Indian Pines and Pavia; ViT is weakest on those, while 1D-CNN does best on Houston.
- Land-use-change analysis (2017->2021) reports a small net increase in forest area for Laibin.

## Relevance to Our Crop-Classification Study

This paper is a near-perfect illustration of the exact pathology our manuscript is built to expose, and is therefore valuable mainly as a **negative exemplar / cautionary citation**. It reports a 97.6% OA crop/land-cover classification using a high-capacity hybrid CNN-RNN, yet its validation is a 10/90 random split of individual pixels with no spatial blocking — and with crop samples drawn from contiguous patches that guarantee adjacent-pixel leakage between train and test. Under our spatially disjoint protocol, scores produced this way are precisely the kind we expect to be inflated and to mis-rank models. The fact that a dense hierarchical CNN-RNN "wins" here by ~1-2 OA points over SpectralFormer and a plain RNN is exactly the brittle, protocol-dependent ranking our work shows can reverse on a holdout tile.

Several specifics map onto our argument. First, the **random-pixel split is the leakage failure mode our FID-disjoint splitting is designed to prevent**; we can cite this paper to motivate why field-wise (and ultimately region-wise) separation is non-negotiable. Second, the authors report only **OA/AA/Kappa, not macro/per-class F1**, on a dataset with severe imbalance (rice has 16 training / 152-162 testing samples vs forest's ~9,000) — so a high OA is dominated by the easy majority classes, masking minority-class behavior. This is the same OA-vs-macro-F1 honesty gap that drives our choice of macro-F1 as primary metric in a lucerne/medics-dominated setting. Third, the result that **dense DL beats classical ML by a thin margin in-scene** is exactly the kind of conclusion our study argues is an artifact of in-region validation; classical sparse models (RF, SVM) here are within ~2 OA of the DL winner and may well transfer better, which their protocol cannot reveal.

The paper is optical-only (Sentinel-2, 13 bands), aligning with our optical-only design, and it is pixel-level — making it a clean comparison case for why pixel-level random splitting is dangerous and why field-level aggregation plus disjoint holdout (our approach) is the corrective.

## Evaluation Caveats

- **Severe spatial leakage.** Random 10/90 pixel split within a single scene, with no field/parcel/region separation; crop samples from contiguous >100 m^2 patches put adjacent, near-identical pixels in both train and test. Reported accuracies are almost certainly upward-biased and do not measure generalization.
- **In-region validation reported as skill.** No spatially disjoint holdout and no cross-year transfer; each year's model is trained and tested within its own scene. This is in-region-random-split, not OOS.
- **Class-imbalance artifact.** Only OA/AA/Kappa reported; no macro-F1 or per-class F1. With forest ~9,000 vs rice ~160 test samples, OA is dominated by majority classes and hides minority-class weakness — the same distortion we avoid by using macro-F1.
- **Thin, protocol-bound model ranking.** The ~1-2 point OA margin by which HCRNN beats SpectralFormer/RNN/classical ML is well within the range our work shows reverses under a disjoint holdout; the "best model" claim is not transfer-validated.
- **Hyperspectral benchmarks share the leakage problem.** Indian Pines/Pavia standard splits are spatially interleaved, so the generalizability claim across datasets does not establish spatial transfer.
- **Land-use-change inference rests on leaky models.** Multi-year area-change conclusions inherit any in-scene optimism of the underlying classifiers.
