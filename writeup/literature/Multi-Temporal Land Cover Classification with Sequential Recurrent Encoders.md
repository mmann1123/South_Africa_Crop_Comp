# Multi-Temporal Land Cover Classification with Sequential Recurrent Encoders

**Citation:** Rußwurm, M., & Körner, M. (2018). Multi-Temporal Land Cover Classification with Sequential Recurrent Encoders. *ISPRS International Journal of Geo-Information*, 7(4), Article 129. DOI: 10.3390/ijgi7040129 (verified via Crossref).
**BibTeX key:** `ruwurm2018multi`

## Objectives

The paper adapts sequence-to-sequence (encoder-decoder) architectures from machine translation and speech recognition to multi-temporal land cover / crop classification on Sentinel-2 (`S2`) image time series. Its central aims are: (1) port sequential encoders from NLP into Earth observation; (2) probe whether a recurrent network can learn an internal, implicit cloud-filtering scheme directly from raw top-of-atmosphere (`TOA`) reflectance, eliminating the explicit atmospheric-correction and cloud-masking preprocessing that conventional multi-temporal pipelines depend on; and (3) demonstrate end-to-end crop classification over a large class set across two growing seasons with minimal preprocessing. This is the key SITS-RNN (satellite image time series, recurrent) comparator for our manuscript: it is the architectural ancestor of the convolutional-recurrent and CNN-BiLSTM models we benchmark, and reference [27] in our own assigned `rustowicz2019` paper extends exactly this C-LSTM line.

## Methods

The model is a bidirectional convolutional recurrent (ConvRNN) encoder. An input image sequence `x = {x_0, ..., x_T}` of shape `h x w x d` is fed to convolutional LSTM or convolutional GRU cells in both forward (`seq`) and reversed (`rev`) order with shared weights, eliminating bias toward late observations. The final cell states `c_T` from both passes are concatenated and projected by a convolutional classification layer to softmax class maps — a many-to-one aggregation producing one areal prediction map for the whole time series. Cross-entropy loss is used. Input feature depth `d = 15`: ten `S2` bands (`B2`,`B3`,`B4`,`B8` at 10 m bilinearly interpolated; `B5`,`B6`,`B7`,`B8A` at 20 m; `B1`,`B11`,`B12` at 60 m) plus year and day-of-year matrices. Crucially the data are atmospherically **uncorrected** `TOA` reflectance, cloud-unfiltered (products with <80% cloud cover retained). Networks use `r ∈ {128, 256}` recurrent cells, kernel `k = 3`; trained 60 epochs on a P100 GPU. The reported evaluation network is the convolutional GRU at `r = 256`. The study area is a 102 km x 42 km region north of Munich, Germany, with 137k field parcels and 17 aggregated crop classes from farmer-declared subsidy records (Bavarian StMELF). The AOI is tiled into 3.84 km blocks **randomly assigned** to training / validation / evaluation partitions in a 4:1:1 ratio.

A distinctive contribution is interpretability: the authors visualize internal LSTM gate activations (input, forget, modulation gates and cell state) across a 36-step sequence and identify a small number of cells (e.g., cell `i = 47`) whose input gate approaches zero on cloudy observations, arguing the network learned internal cloud masking.

**Evaluation protocol:** Spatial-block-wise split — the AOI is subdivided into 3.84 km square blocks that are *randomly* assigned to train/val/test partitions "to ensure dataset independence." This is stronger than pooled-pixel k-fold and provides genuine spatial separation between train and test blocks (a coarse block-holdout), but it is **single-scene, single-region, within-AOI** randomization, not a spatially disjoint distant tile and not cross-year transfer (both 2016 and 2017 seasons appear in training and evaluation). It sits between within-scene field-wise k-fold and a true disjoint holdout on our protocol spectrum. Metrics are computed **pixel-wise** (precision/recall/f-measure per class) — predictions are not aggregated to field level for scoring, so spatial autocorrelation among pixels within a block inflates apparent performance relative to field-level scoring.

## Key Findings

The convolutional GRU achieved 89.7% overall accuracy and overall kappa 0.870 in 2016, and 89.5% OA / 0.870 kappa in 2017, on raw `TOA` data with no atmospheric correction or cloud masking. Pixel-averaged precision/recall/f-score ranged 89.3–89.9%. The authors report conditional (class-wise) kappa to compensate for the strongly non-uniform class distribution (maize and meadow dominate; peas, summer oat, winter spelt are rare). Class-wise f-measures varied widely: 96.8% for maize (the most frequent class) down to 41.5% recall / 0.414 conditional kappa for peas (the rarest). Several confusions (triticale↔wheat, barley↔triticale) were consistent across both seasons (spectral/phenological similarity), while others (peas↔meadow/potato in 2017 only) were season-specific, which the authors attribute to variable phenological onset driven by weather. Gate-activation visualization provided qualitative evidence of learned internal cloud filtering. Compared against prior hand-crafted approaches (HMM, RF, OBIA+DT) in their Table 3, the ConvRNN matched state-of-the-art accuracy with a larger class count and no preprocessing.

## Relevance to Our Crop-Classification Study

This is a foundational reference for the deep-temporal branch of our model comparison. It establishes the convolutional-recurrent / C-LSTM family that our CNN-BiLSTM and L-TAE models descend from, and it is the canonical demonstration that a recurrent net can learn phenological structure end-to-end from raw `S2` time series. Several design choices align directly with ours: `S2` multi-temporal optical input, the inclusion of SWIR bands (`B11`,`B12`) and `B2`/`B6` that overlap our band set, optical-only (no SAR), and explicit use of a **balanced metric (kappa, conditional kappa)** to counter class imbalance — the same motivation behind our macro-F1 primary metric and our lucerne/medics-dominated distribution. The class-imbalance behavior they report (rare peas collapsing to 0.414 kappa while dominant maize hits 0.968) directly prefigures our per-class instability for minority crops.

Most importantly, this paper is an exemplar of the validation gap our manuscript exposes. Its headline ~90% OA / 0.87 kappa is obtained on randomly assigned blocks *within a single 102 x 42 km AOI*, scored *pixel-wise*, on data spanning the same two years used in training. There is no spatially disjoint distant holdout and no field-level aggregation for scoring. Our central finding — that dense temporal deep nets that look excellent under in-region validation collapse out-of-sample, and that field-level aggregation partly rescues them — predicts that this network's transfer to a disjoint tile would be substantially weaker than its reported in-region numbers suggest. The pixel-wise scoring in particular means spatial autocorrelation within blocks is doing hidden work; our field-wise (FID-disjoint) splitting and our disjoint `34S_20E_259N` holdout are precisely the controls absent here. This paper should be cited as the methodological baseline whose evaluation regime we tighten.

## Evaluation Caveats

- **Spatial leakage / separation:** Block-wise random assignment gives partial spatial separation (train and test blocks are distinct), better than pooled k-fold, but all blocks come from one contiguous AOI with shared climate, soils, sensor geometry, and farming practices. There is no distant disjoint tile, so the result does not measure geographic transfer. Flag: in-AOI block-holdout, not true OOS.
- **Pixel-level scoring, no field aggregation:** Metrics are pixel-wise. Pixels within a field/block are highly autocorrelated, so reported accuracy overstates field-level performance. The paper does NOT report field-level (per-FID) accuracy — a silence that matters for comparison with field-aggregated scores.
- **No cross-year holdout:** Both 2016 and 2017 are present in training and evaluation; the season-specific confusions the authors flag are warning signs that cross-year transfer would degrade, but this is never tested.
- **Class imbalance:** Handled correctly via conditional/overall kappa alongside OA; the authors explicitly note OA is skewed toward frequent classes. Good practice, mirrors our balanced-metric stance.
- **Cloud-masking claim is qualitative:** The "learned internal cloud filtering" conclusion rests on visual inspection of a handful of LSTM cells, not a controlled ablation; treat as suggestive, not proven.
- **What it does not measure:** geographic transfer, cross-sensor transfer, field-level macro-F1, and robustness of the cloud-masking behavior under distribution shift — all directly relevant gaps our study addresses.
