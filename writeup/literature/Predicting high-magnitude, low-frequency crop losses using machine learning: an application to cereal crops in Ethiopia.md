# Predicting high-magnitude, low-frequency crop losses using machine learning: an application to cereal crops in Ethiopia

**Citation:** Mann, M. L., Warner, J. M., & Malik, A. S. (2019). Predicting high-magnitude, low-frequency crop losses using machine learning: an application to cereal crops in Ethiopia. *Climatic Change*, 154(1–2), 211–227. DOI: 10.1007/s10584-019-02432-7 (verified via Crossref).
**BibTeX key:** `mann2019predicting`

> **Authorship note:** The lead author (Michael L. Mann, GWU Geography) is the same researcher as our manuscript's author. This is a self-relevant methodological precedent — the time-series feature-extraction tooling (`ts-raster`, the precursor to `xr_fresh`) and the random-forest / phenology-summary approach used here are direct ancestors of our pipeline's feature-engineering stage.

## Objectives

Develop a scalable, replicable machine-learning method to **forecast substantial (≥25%) crop losses from drought** at fine spatial resolution (sub-kebele, ~200 households / ~24 km²) by mid-growing-season, using **only remotely sensed data** (vegetation, precipitation, evapotranspiration). The motivation is humanitarian early warning: existing loss reporting is aggregated to zonal/regional levels, masking large village-level variability; the paper aims to recover that fine-scale signal and predict it *before* the season ends to enable targeted intervention. Applied to five principal Ethiopian cereal crops — maize, wheat, sorghum, barley, teff. This is a **crop-loss / yield-anomaly forecasting** paper, not a crop-type classification paper, so the relevance is methodological (feature engineering, RF, evaluation discipline, balanced metrics) rather than task-identical.

## Methods

A **data-fusion** design couples ground-truth crop-loss survey data with remotely sensed time series. Survey: Ethiopia's Agricultural Sample Survey (AgSS, Central Statistical Agency; ~45,000 households), farmer-reported percent crop production lost to drought, aggregated to sub-kebele level, 2010–2015 (five favorable seasons 2010–2014 plus the 2015 drought year), ~10,680 observations. Remote sensing: (1) `NDVI` from 250 m MODIS 16-day products (MOD13Q1 + MYD13Q1, Aqua+Terra combined); (2) precipitation from CHIRPS (dekadal); (3) potential and actual evapotranspiration (PET, AET/ETA anomaly). The signature contribution is **temporal-feature summarization**: reconciling high-frequency 8-day/dekadal series with the low-frequency seasonal loss event by extracting **41 phenological metrics** per crop — summary statistics (mean, max, variance), integrated statistics (area-under-curve for the *leading half* of the season), and deviation-from-norm statistics (departures from historical percentiles). Critically, model inputs are **restricted to data up to the midpoint of the season** (estimated planting date through date of peak greenness) to make the forecast genuinely *predictive* rather than retrospective. Estimated planting date = global NDVI minimum in the planting window; peak greenness = season NDVI maximum. The classifier is a **random forest** predicting a binary target `Y_ci = 1 if drought loss ≥ 0.25`. Spatial autocorrelation is explicitly handled in training/cross-validation (described in the appendix). Tooling released open-source (`ts-raster` Python package; GitHub repo).

**Evaluation protocol:** **Out-of-sample** prediction with confusion matrices and three metrics — overall accuracy, **Cohen's kappa**, and **recall** — per crop. The split is described as out-of-sample with explicit handling of spatial autocorrelation in the cross-validation scheme (so train/test are not naively pooled across autocorrelated neighbors). This is a panel over 2010–2015 across the cereal-growing regions of Ethiopia; the design forecasts a *future, rare* event (the 2015 drought) from prior-season phenology, giving it a temporal-holdout flavor in addition to spatial cross-validation. It is **not** a single-scene k-fold; the authors foreground recall over accuracy precisely because of severe class imbalance, and they correct for spatial leakage — both unusually disciplined for the genre.

## Key Findings

Out-of-sample recall for correctly flagging substantial (≥25%) drought-loss sub-kebeles ranged **57–81%, median 65%**, with overall accuracy 94–97% and kappa 0.60–0.81 across the five crops. Per-crop: maize accuracy 0.96 / kappa 0.68 / recall 0.81; wheat 0.97 / 0.71 / 0.65; sorghum 0.97 / 0.81 / 0.75; barley 0.94 / 0.62 / 0.58; teff 0.95 / 0.60 / 0.57. **The authors explicitly warn that high overall accuracy is misleading under class imbalance** — because substantial-loss events are rare, a trivial "all healthy" classifier already scores ~95% accuracy, so they emphasize recall and kappa as the meaningful metrics. Performance was best for early-planted crops (maize, sorghum, mean recall 84%) and weaker for late-planted crops (barley, teff, wheat ~60%), implying salient temporal features were missing for late-season crops — directly motivating better time-series feature extraction (the future `xr_fresh`/`ts-raster` work). Partial-dependence plots showed meaningful, nonlinear, crop-shared relationships: date-of-maximum-greenness and its deviation from the median, planting-date deviations, leading-half cumulative precipitation, and 90th-percentile NDVI and its first-difference were the most influential variables (U-shaped responses; both drought and excess precipitation raise loss probability). The authors note prospective improvement from 30 m Harmonized Landsat Sentinel (HLS) data once available — finer resolution and 5-day cadence to beat cloud contamination and the coarse 250 m MODIS cells.

## Relevance to Our Crop-Classification Study

The strongest link is methodological lineage and evaluation discipline. This paper is the proving ground for the **time-series phenological feature engineering** that becomes our `xr_fresh` features — extracting summary, integrated (AUC), and deviation-from-norm statistics from multi-temporal optical signals, then feeding a sparse tree ensemble. It validates the "sparse, feature-selecting, tree-like model on engineered temporal features" recipe that our manuscript finds transfers best, on a real, hard, imbalanced task. Several practices we adopt are pre-figured here: **random forest / tree-based classifier**, **explicit class-imbalance handling via balanced metrics (kappa, recall) rather than raw accuracy**, **explicit spatial-autocorrelation control in cross-validation** (the kin of our FID-disjoint splitting against leakage), and forecasting/scoring **out-of-sample**. The blunt warning that 95% accuracy is an artifact of a rare positive class is exactly the lesson our lucerne/medics-dominated, macro-F1-primary design encodes.

Differences to flag in citing it: the task is **drought-loss forecasting, not crop-type classification**, so its accuracy/kappa numbers are not directly comparable to our per-crop macro-F1; it operates at **coarse 250 m MODIS** resolution (vs our 10–20 m `S2`), uses **MODIS NDVI + CHIRPS precipitation + ET** (not our `S2` `B2`,`B6`,`B11`,`B12`,`EVI`,`hue` band stack), and fuses **survey ground truth** rather than parcel labels. It is also optical+climate (no SAR), aligning with our optical-only stance. Use it to justify our feature-engineering choices, our tree-based classical baselines, our balanced-metric reporting, and our anti-leakage cross-validation — and as evidence from the same author group that careful temporal feature design plus out-of-sample, imbalance-aware evaluation is the right methodological frame.

## Evaluation Caveats

- **Class imbalance handled exemplarily:** The authors lead with recall and kappa and explicitly disclaim overall accuracy — best-in-class practice for our manuscript's concerns and a model for how we frame our minority-crop results.
- **Spatial leakage controlled:** Spatial autocorrelation is explicitly addressed in cross-validation, mitigating the leakage that would otherwise inflate out-of-sample scores; this is closer to a true holdout than naive k-fold.
- **Temporal-holdout flavor, but limited drought years:** The headline rare-event (2015 drought) prediction rests on a single severe-drought year against five favorable years; the positive class is small, so kappa/recall have wide effective uncertainty (the authors acknowledge "only having a few observations" for some response shapes).
- **Coarse resolution / cloud exposure:** 250 m MODIS cells aggregate heterogeneous smallholder fields; the authors flag this as a key limitation and look to 30 m HLS for improvement — a reminder that resolution and cloud contamination bound the achievable signal (relevant to our months-05/06 exclusion).
- **Survey-label noise:** Ground truth is farmer-reported loss, which the authors note may conflate drought with other causes — an irreducible label-noise floor absent from our parcel-label setting.
- **What it does not measure:** crop *type* discrimination, fine-resolution (`S2`-scale) performance, cross-region transfer beyond Ethiopia, and any macro-F1 — its metrics are accuracy/kappa/recall for a binary loss target.
