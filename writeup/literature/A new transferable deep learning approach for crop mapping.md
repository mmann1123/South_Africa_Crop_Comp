# A new transferable deep learning approach for crop mapping

**Citation:** Hongyan Che, Yaozhong Pan, Xingsheng Xia, Xiufang Zhu, Le Li, Yongsheng Huang, Xuechang Zheng, and Lingang Wang (2024). *GIScience & Remote Sensing* 61(1), 2395700. DOI: `10.1080/15481603.2024.2395700` (verified: extracted from the PDF first page; Crossref returns the identical title, volume 61, article 2395700, 2024). Open access (CC BY-NC). Code: https://github.com/Maria-hyChe/DSH (see Evaluation Caveats).

## Objectives

The paper proposes DSH, a pixel-wise semantic segmentation network built from three parts: a DeepLabV3+ backbone (MobileNetV2 encoder, ASPP), a channel self-attention module (a six-layer Transformer encoder run over the backbone's feature channels), and a histogram-matching step that rewrites each test image's band histograms to resemble a reference training image. The input is a single monthly Sentinel-2 composite, so the stated aim is a crop mapper that is temporally, spatially and spatiotemporally transferable while needing little input data. Five questions are posed: how temporal transferability changes with the growth season, how full spatial transfer compares with transfer among sites of similar planting structure, how spatiotemporal transfer varies by region, how DSH compares with HRNetV2, DeepLabV3+ and random forest, and whether one peak-season month matches a full May to October series.

## Methods

**Study sites (Sec. 2.1).** Eight sites, each a 35 to 55 km square: Sites 1 to 3 in the central United States (Minnesota, Nebraska, Arkansas), Sites 4 to 6 in Heilongjiang, China, Sites 7 to 8 in Normandy and Brittany, France. All are single-season systems (maize, soybean, rice, sunflower, tuber/root crops). Sites 1 to 2 and 7 to 8 are paired as "similar planting structure"; Sites 3 to 6 mix maize, rice and soybean in different proportions. Landscapes are large and regular in Sites 1 to 4 and fragmented in Sites 5 to 8.

**Labels (Sec. 2.1).** Not field surveys. Sites 1 to 3 use the USDA Cropland Data Layer, Sites 4 to 6 use National Bureau of Statistics of China data, and Sites 7 to 8 use Theia's land-cover product. Classes are collapsed to 3 or 4 crop classes plus "others" (other crops, water, buildings, forest, meadow). The authors note they used the CDL "directly, without implementing a further selection process."

**Imagery (Sec. 2.2).** Sentinel-2 Level-2A surface reflectance in Google Earth Engine, ten bands (B, G, R, RE1 to RE4, NIR, SWIR1, SWIR2) at 10 m. Monthly composites were built "using the quality Mosaic function with the normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) as a quality control band" for May 1 to October 30, 2019 to 2022. No cloud-mask product, threshold or gap-filling step is described. Table 1 lists scene counts per site and year (for example Site 1: 110, 113, 110, 111 scenes for 2019 to 2022; Site 6: 74, 77, 73, 74).

**Training (Sec. 3.3).** Training data come from 2019 only, per site. Images are cut into 256 x 256 pixel blocks with 50 percent overlap (about 2,000 to 2,600 blocks per site), blocks with under 10 percent crop area are dropped, and 90 percent of blocks are randomly assigned to training and 10 percent to validation. Cross-entropy loss is used at Sites 1 to 2 and focal loss at Sites 3 to 8. SGD, 123 epochs, batch 64. RF is tuned over tree counts with at most 10 features. One DSH model is trained per site per month, so the six monthly models are never combined.

**Histogram matching (Sec. 3.1.3).** Both images are histogram-equalised and the test image is mapped through the inverse of the training image's equalisation, band by band. This is transductive: it uses test-image statistics at inference time.

**Experiments (Sec. 3.4).** (1) Temporal transfer: train in month m of 2019 at a site, test on month m of 2020 to 2022 at the same site. (2) Spatial transfer in 2019: full transfer from Site 1 to Sites 2 to 8, and similar-structure transfer (1 to 2, 2 to 1, 7 to 8, 8 to 7, and 3 versus 4 to 6). (3) Spatiotemporal transfer: train 2019 peak month at one site, test 2020 to 2022 at a similar-structure site. (4) Baselines on 2019 train and 2020 test. (5) Peak month (August) versus full May to October series at Site 2.

**Metrics (Sec. 3.5).** Overall accuracy (OA), producer and user accuracy (PA, UA) and per-class F1. No macro-F1 or Kappa is reported as a summary.

**Evaluation protocol.** Cross-year, cross-site and cross-year-plus-cross-site transfer, scored pixel-wise against the same CDL-type label products, with "others" (water, forest, buildings) counted in OA. This sits at the transfer end of our spectrum, but two points soften it. The "temporal transfer" test is on the same site and the same fields one to three years later, so it is a cross-year, same-footprint test, not a spatial holdout, and field identity is not controlled. The random 90/10 block split inside each training site is only used for validation, not for any reported headline score. The spatial-transfer tests are genuinely disjoint by site, but they are chosen after seeing that full transfer works worse (Sec. 4.2: "we elected to conduct it between sites with similar planting structures"), so the headline spatiotemporal score is on the favourable pairings.

## Key Findings

Temporal transfer accuracy rises through the season, peaks in August, and falls afterwards (Fig. 6). In August OA exceeds 90 percent at Sites 1, 2, 4, 7 and 8 and is near 85 percent at the other three; in May most sites are at or below 70 percent. Averaged over eight sites and three years the August model reaches 90.9 percent OA (Sec. 4.1); Site 7 is best at 97 percent and Site 6 worst at 84.3 percent. Per-class PA averages are 86.3 percent maize, 85.1 rice, 62.9 tubers/roots, 55.5 sunflower and 92.6 soybean, and the authors link the weak classes to training shares under 1 percent.

Full spatial transfer from Site 1 (2019, Table 2) gives OA of 94.4 (Site 2), 83.7 (3), 85.9 (4), 75.6 (5), 71.3 (6), 90.0 (7) and 89.3 (8). Restricting to similar planting structures improves 6 of 7 sites (Site 6 by 9.9 points; Site 3 falls 2.1). Spatiotemporal transfer averages 88.1 percent OA, with drops relative to temporal transfer of 2.4 and 0.7 points at Sites 1 and 2, roughly 4 to 5 points at Sites 3 to 6, and none at Sites 7 to 8 (Fig. 10).

Against baselines (2020, peak month) DSH reaches 91.2 percent average OA versus HRNetV2 87.6, DeepLabV3+ 86.6 and RF 83.0. Peak-month and full-season DSH are equivalent (93.86 versus 93.93 percent mean OA at Site 2, Table 3).

Histogram matching is the main transfer finding. With matching, OA is stable across 2020 to 2022 (averages 90.9 OA, 84.8 PA, 84.0 UA). Without it, 2022 collapses to 70.8 OA, 62.5 PA and 59.4 UA, with every site below 80 percent OA (Fig. 15). The authors trace this to a Copernicus change: "after 25 January 2022, S2_SR images had their DN value range shifted by 1000," which moves the raw histograms of 2022 away from 2019 (Fig. 14). Channel self-attention helps most when inserted at the high-level features (DSH_P2: 91.3 and 94.0 OA at Sites 1 and 2 versus 88.7 and 92.1 for plain DeepLabV3+, Table 4).

## Relevance to Our Crop-Classification Study

It is a transfer study, which is rare in this corpus, but it points in a different direction from ours. The model is a heavy segmentation network (DeepLabV3+ plus a Transformer), the setting is large-field single-season crops, and the headline transfer scores are 88 to 91 percent OA with a dominant "others" class. Our finding that sparse, tree-like models transfer best and dense temporal nets overfit is not tested here: the only non-deep baseline is RF, which is the worst model, but it is trained on a single peak month per pixel and is not tuned like our gradient-boosted trees, so it neither supports nor contradicts us.

Three things are directly useful. First, the paper's strongest result is that most of its cross-year loss is a radiometric artefact (the January 2022 offset in `S2_SR`), not a biological or spatial shift, and that a test-time distribution-alignment step removes it. That is a concrete reminder that a "transfer drop" can be a preprocessing change, which is the same logic as our holdout September fill check. Second, the peak-month finding (one August composite matches six months of data) echoes our observation that most of the signal sits in a few informative dates, and argues for checking how much of our temporal input is redundant. Third, histogram matching is a candidate low-cost control for our holdout tile if its band distributions differ from the training tiles, though it rewrites inputs at inference and would need to be applied to the tabular xr_fresh features in some form, not to rasters.

It is also relevant to the missingness thread. The paper builds monthly composites without describing a cloud mask or any treatment of months with no clear view, never reports valid-observation counts per site or year, and states that DSH "might be ineffective" when images are "heavily polluted by clouds and shadows, and even missing." That limitation is acknowledged only in the future-work paragraph and is not tested.

## Evaluation Caveats

Labels are derived products (CDL, Theia land cover, Chinese statistics), not field surveys, so the reference carries its own error, and the authors acknowledge noisy CDL samples. Accuracy is pixel-wise OA, dominated in several sites by the "others" class and by one or two large crops; OA is reported alongside PA and UA but no macro-F1, and the weak classes (sunflower, tubers/roots at 55 to 63 percent PA) are small enough that OA hides them ("the low PA and UA ... had no effect on the OA, due to their minimal planting proportion").

Temporal transfer re-tests the same footprint, so spatial autocorrelation with the training pixels is not removed; the spatial transfers are disjoint but the paper selects the similar-structure pairings after seeing the full-transfer results. Spatial transfer is one-to-one (Site 1 as the base for all, or a single partner), not a leave-one-region-out design across eight sites. Histogram matching uses statistics of the test image, so the model is not applied to a target without access to target data, which is acceptable operationally but is not the same claim as an unadapted transfer; the paper never reports unadapted spatial transfer with and without matching, only the cross-year comparison in Fig. 15.

Not measured: per-domain cloud or valid-observation fraction, the share of pixels whose composite relies on a single scene, the effect of the compositing rule on the transfer drop, and any computational cost comparison beyond parameter remarks. Early-season accuracy (May at or below 70 percent) is attributed to phenology and sowing-date variation without a missingness check.

The code repository exists (`nets/`, `utils/`, `train.py`, `predict.py`, `match_histograms.py`, `annotation.py`), but the page listing shows no trained weights, no dataset files and no Earth Engine export script, so the composites and sites are not reproducible from the repository alone. The data availability statement points to Google Earth Engine, CropScape and Theia.
