# Crop Classification from Satellite Image Time Series: A Literature Review

## 1. Introduction and scope

Mapping crop types from satellite image time series has become one of the central applied problems in agricultural remote sensing, underpinning yield forecasting, food-security monitoring, and land-use accounting. The free, frequent, multispectral acquisitions of Sentinel-2 — and before it the MODIS and Landsat archives — have made dense intra-seasonal observation routine, and a large methodological literature now spans classical machine learning on engineered features, deep sequence and convolutional models, and patch-based spatial architectures. This review synthesizes that literature with a deliberate orientation toward the conditions that make the problem hardest: the dryland and smallholder farming systems of the Global South, where the gap between a model's reported accuracy and its behavior on new terrain is widest and most consequential.

The orientation matters because most of the field's headline results are obtained, implicitly or explicitly, under conditions that flatter the model. The recurring methodological fault line traced through this review is the distinction between *in-distribution fit* — accuracy measured on held-out pixels or fields drawn from the same scene, season, and sensor geometry as the training data — and *spatial transfer*, the ability to classify a region where no labels were collected, which is what operational crop mapping actually requires. As the sections below show, the two are routinely conflated, and the conflation systematically distorts which models the literature recommends.

## 2. Challenges to crop separability

Several physical and agronomic factors make crops genuinely difficult to separate from optical imagery, and they recur across study sites. The first is spectral similarity among phenologically adjacent crops: cereals such as wheat, barley, and grazing grasses share reflectance trajectories closely enough that single-date imagery cannot reliably resolve them, and even multi-date analyses succeed only within specific phenological windows. Foundational separability work on MODIS vegetation-index time series over the U.S. Great Plains established that crops become statistically distinguishable only during particular growth stages rather than across the whole season ([Wardlow et al., MODIS Great Plains](<Analysis of time-series MODIS 250 m vegetation index data for crop classification in the U.S. Central Great Plains.md>)), and object-based work over Yolo County, California, reached the same conclusion — late-summer imagery carried the majority of the discriminating power, with spectral indices supplying most of the signal and texture contributing only marginally ([Peña-Barragán et al. 2011](<Object-based crop identification using multiple vegetation indices, textural features and crop phenology.md>)). Smallholder studies sharpen the point: single- versus intercropped maize in Kenya separated cleanly only during mid-season flowering, collapsing to chance at other stages ([Characterising maize signatures, Kenya](<Characterising maize and intercropped maize spectral signatures for cropping pattern classification.md>)).

The second factor is phenological timing itself. Planting calendars vary across fields, seasons, and sub-regions, so the same crop presents different temporal signatures depending on when it was sown; the Great Plains analysis documented roughly month-long phenological offsets across a single state ([Wardlow et al.](<Analysis of time-series MODIS 250 m vegetation index data for crop classification in the U.S. Central Great Plains.md>)). This intra-class temporal variability is precisely the mechanism by which a classifier tuned to one region's calendar misfires on another's. Third, cloud cover — endemic in tropical and rain-fed systems — produces irregular, gap-ridden optical sequences that must be interpolated or masked, a problem repeatedly flagged as the binding constraint on operational mapping in Sub-Saharan Africa ([Bégué et al., RS for Africa policy](<begue2020_RS_Africa_policy.md>)). Finally, smallholder field size and heterogeneity compress the spatial signal: fields a few pixels across leave little room for spatial-context models to work, mixed pixels dominate, and SAR fusion that helps over large fields stops helping over small ones ([Rustowicz et al. 2019](<rustowicz2019_CropType_Africa.md>)). The Western Cape, with its large commercial fields, is comparatively tractable on the last axis, which makes it a useful but not worst-case setting ([Bégué et al.](<begue2020_RS_Africa_policy.md>)).

## 3. Modeling approaches

The classical era of the field rests on tree ensembles and margin classifiers operating on engineered inputs. Random Forest in particular became the remote-sensing workhorse, valued for handling high dimensionality and multicollinearity, requiring few tuning decisions, and resisting overfitting as trees are added — properties traceable to Breiman's strength-and-correlation generalization theory ([Breiman 2001](<breiman2001_RandomForests.md>)) and consolidated for the remote-sensing community in a widely cited review ([Belgiu & Drăguț 2016](<Random forest in remote sensing: A review of applications and future.md>)). Gradient-boosted trees — formalized as stagewise function-space descent ([Friedman, Greedy Function Approximation](<GREEDY FUNCTION APPROXIMATION:.md>)) and engineered for scale in XGBoost ([Chen & Guestrin 2016](<xgboost_chen2016.md>)) and LightGBM ([Ke et al. 2017](<ke2017_LightGBM_NeurIPS.md>)) — share the same axis-aligned, implicitly feature-selecting inductive bias. Comparative and applied reviews find no universal winner among classical methods, with rankings shifting between SVM and RF depending on data and protocol, but consistently report tree ensembles as robust to small samples, label noise, and reduced training fractions ([Maxwell et al., applied review](<Implementation of machine-learning classification in remote sensing  an applied review.md>); [Belgiu & Drăguț 2016](<Random forest in remote sensing: A review of applications and future.md>)). On single-date Sentinel-2, RF edged SVM for crop and land-cover mapping ([Saini & Ghosh 2018](<saini2018_RF_SVM_Sentinel2.md>)).

Deep learning entered the field on the premise that learned representations should capture phenological dynamics that hand-crafted summaries miss. Recurrent encoders — LSTM and bidirectional convolutional-recurrent variants — were among the first, modeling the temporal sequence directly ([Ienco et al. 2017](<ieee2017_Ienco_RNN.md>); [Rußwurm & Körner, sequential recurrent encoders](<Multi-Temporal Land Cover Classification with Sequential Recurrent Encoders.md>)). Temporal convolution offered a feed-forward alternative in TempCNN ([Pelletier et al., TempCNN](<Temporal Convolutional Neural Network for the Classification of Satellite Image Time Series.md>)), and temporal self-attention culminated in the Lightweight Temporal Attention Encoder (L-TAE), a compact parcel-level model that outperformed recurrent, convolutional, and transformer baselines on a French benchmark with a fraction of the parameters ([Garnot & Landrieu, L-TAE](<ltae2020_Garnot_LTAE.md>)). A parallel line treats each pixel's multi-temporal vector as tabular input: TabNet brings deep learning to that setting through sequential *sparsemax* attentive masks that select a small feature subset per instance, rivaling boosted trees on tabular benchmarks ([Arık & Pfister, TabNet](<tabnet_orig_Arik.md>)) — a mechanism rooted in the sparsemax operator itself ([Martins & Astudillo 2016](<From Softmax to Sparsemax - A Sparse Model of Attention and Multi-Label Classification.md>)). Patch and spatial models — 2D/3D CNNs, U-Net segmentation networks, and convolutional-recurrent hybrids — add spatial context, and hybrid pipelines proliferate: CNN-RNN stacks ([Teixeira et al., red-edge CNN-RNN](<Deep Learning Applications for Crop Mapping Using Multi-Temporal Sentinel-2 Data and Red-Edge Vegetation Indices: Integrating Convolutional and Recurrent Neural Networks.md>); [attention-BiLSTM + TempCNN](<bandar2024_BiLSTM_TCN.md>)), CNN feature extractors feeding classical heads — a pattern that recurs in close-range crop imagery, where swapping a dense softmax head for a margin or boosted-tree classifier reins in overfitting on scarce data ([CNN-SVM grapevine](<A CNN-SVM study based on selected deep features for grapevine leaves classification.md>); [CNN-RF hybrid](<Crop Classification Method Based on Optimal Feature Selection and Hybrid CNN-RF Networks for Multi-Temporal Remote Sensing Imagery.md>); [weed identification via transfer learning](<Towards weeds identification assistance through transfer learning.md>); [crop/disease ID, shallow vs AlexNet](<Crop identification and disease classification using traditional machine learning and deep learning approaches.md>)), and transformer-convolution combinations with self-supervised pre-training ([Cropformer 2023](<cropformer2023_Wang.md>)). Broad surveys catalog this taxonomy and its growth ([Ball et al. 2017 DL survey](<ball2017_DL_RemoteSensing_survey.md>); [DL crops aerial-imagery review](<Deep Learning Models for the Classification of Crops in Aerial Imagery: A Review.md>)).

A persistent disagreement runs through this body of work: does deep learning actually beat classical methods? In-region benchmarks overwhelmingly say yes — one systematic review found deep nets outperformed classical baselines in 35 of 36 studies, the lone exception being a small-training-set case ([DL crops aerial-imagery review](<Deep Learning Models for the Classification of Crops in Aerial Imagery: A Review.md>)). But the margins are often thin (a Landsat-EVI comparison gave the best 1D-CNN only ~1.4 accuracy points over the best tree model, with LSTM the *worst* performer; [Cai et al., DL multitemporal](<Deep learning based multi-temporal crop classification☆.md>)), the rankings among temporal nets are unstable across datasets, and — as Section 5 argues — the comparison is almost always made under the protocol that most favors high-capacity models.

## 4. Representation and features

Three representational strategies compete. The first is hand-crafted descriptors — vegetation indices, phenological metrics, and statistical summaries — fed to classical learners. This approach dominated early work and remains strong: decision trees on object-level VI and texture features achieved competitive accuracy while aggressively selecting a handful of variables from hundreds ([Peña-Barragán et al. 2011](<Object-based crop identification using multiple vegetation indices, textural features and crop phenology.md>)), and phenological-metric pipelines on coarse time series support operational forecasting ([Mann et al., Ethiopia crop loss](<Predicting high-magnitude, low-frequency crop losses using machine learning: an application to cereal crops in Ethiopia.md>)). A recurring empirical finding is that raw spectral bands — especially red-edge and SWIR — often carry as much or more discriminating power than derived indices ([Characterising maize signatures](<Characterising maize and intercropped maize spectral signatures for cropping pattern classification.md>); [CNN-RF hybrid](<Crop Classification Method Based on Optimal Feature Selection and Hybrid CNN-RF Networks for Multi-Temporal Remote Sensing Imagery.md>); [Pelletier et al.](<Temporal Convolutional Neural Network for the Classification of Satellite Image Time Series.md>)), and that spectral indices tend to transfer across regions more stably than raw bands ([Belgiu & Drăguț 2016](<Random forest in remote sensing: A review of applications and future.md>)). The second strategy is *automated* time-series feature extraction, which mechanizes the construction of large statistical feature banks from temporal sequences — the lineage behind the present study's use of `xr_fresh` ([Mann et al., Ethiopia](<Predicting high-magnitude, low-frequency crop losses using machine learning: an application to cereal crops in Ethiopia.md>)). The third is end-to-end learned representations, in which the network discovers its own features from raw reflectance sequences or image patches ([Garnot & Landrieu](<ltae2020_Garnot_LTAE.md>); [Rußwurm & Körner](<Multi-Temporal Land Cover Classification with Sequential Recurrent Encoders.md>); [Cropformer 2023](<cropformer2023_Wang.md>)).

Granularity is the orthogonal design axis. Pixel-level models are most common but most exposed to spatial autocorrelation; field- or parcel-level aggregation pools pixels into a single vector per object and is both a leakage control and a variance-reduction device, exemplified by the Pixel-Set Encoder feeding L-TAE ([Garnot & Landrieu](<ltae2020_Garnot_LTAE.md>)) and by object-based decision-tree pipelines ([Peña-Barragán et al. 2011](<Object-based crop identification using multiple vegetation indices, textural features and crop phenology.md>)). Patch-level models trade leakage risk for spatial context and dominate the segmentation literature ([U-Net Kenya CV4A](<Satellite Imagery Analysis for Crop Type Segmentation Using U-Net Architecture.md>); [3D-ConvSTAR optical-SAR fusion](<Enhanced crop classification through integrated optical and SAR data  a deep learning approach for multi-source image fusion.md>)). The assumption that richer learned representations should generalize better than compact engineered ones is widespread but, as the next section shows, poorly tested under genuine spatial transfer.

A second representational debate concerns sensor fusion. Combining optical with SAR repeatedly improves in-scene accuracy — a Landsat-plus-Sentinel-1 SVM gained roughly twelve accuracy points over single-sensor inputs in cloud-prone Iran ([optical-SAR phenology fusion](<Crop classification based on phenology information by using time series of optical and synthetic-aperture radar images.md>)), and SAR proved especially useful for cropland delineation at national scale ([Jin et al., GEE smallholder maize](<Smallholder maize area and yield mapping at national scales with Google Earth Engine.md>)). Yet the benefit is not universal: SAR did not reliably help over the small fields of smallholder Africa ([Rustowicz et al. 2019](<rustowicz2019_CropType_Africa.md>)), and most fusion gains are reported under leakage-prone within-scene protocols, making them upper bounds rather than transfer-validated improvements ([optical-SAR phenology fusion](<Crop classification based on phenology information by using time series of optical and synthetic-aperture radar images.md>)).

## 5. Evaluation practices and their pitfalls

This is where the literature is weakest, and where a clear-eyed audit pays off. Across the corpus, the dominant evaluation protocol is a random or stratified hold-out drawn from a single scene and season — pooled-pixel k-fold at the weakest end, field- or polygon-disjoint k-fold somewhat better. The crucial property all of these share is that they hold out *samples* but not *space*: training and test data share soils, atmospheric conditions, management calendars, and acquisition geometry, so the reported accuracy reflects in-distribution fit, not transfer.

The most serious version of the problem is spatial leakage. When neighboring, spatially autocorrelated pixels from the same field land on both sides of a random split, accuracy is inflated without measuring anything generalizable. Several otherwise careful crop and land-cover studies do exactly this — random pixel splits with no spatial blocking ([Saini & Ghosh 2018](<saini2018_RF_SVM_Sentinel2.md>); [HCRNN, Sentinel-2 Laibin](<forests-14-01881.md>); [Ienco et al. 2017](<ieee2017_Ienco_RNN.md>); [CNN-RF hybrid](<Crop Classification Method Based on Optimal Feature Selection and Hybrid CNN-RF Networks for Multi-Temporal Remote Sensing Imagery.md>)) — and the most extreme cases report accuracies near 98% under configurations where crop samples are drawn from contiguous patches that straddle the split ([HCRNN](<forests-14-01881.md>); [multimodal NAIP-MODIS fusion](<Multimodal Deep Learning Based Crop Classification Using Multispectral and Multitemporal Satellite Imagery.md>)). Field-wise (FID-disjoint) splitting is the minimum remedy and is increasingly adopted ([Pelletier et al.](<Temporal Convolutional Neural Network for the Classification of Satellite Image Time Series.md>); [Teixeira et al.](<Deep Learning Applications for Crop Mapping Using Multi-Temporal Sentinel-2 Data and Red-Edge Vegetation Indices: Integrating Convolutional and Recurrent Neural Networks.md>); [Garnot & Landrieu](<ltae2020_Garnot_LTAE.md>); [Peña-Barragán et al. 2011](<Object-based crop identification using multiple vegetation indices, textural features and crop phenology.md>)), but it does not separate train and test in space; parcels remain interleaved across a single footprint. A few studies climb further up the ladder — spatial-block assignment ([Rußwurm & Körner](<Multi-Temporal Land Cover Classification with Sequential Recurrent Encoders.md>)) and 10-km grid-disjoint grouping ([3D-ConvSTAR](<Enhanced crop classification through integrated optical and SAR data  a deep learning approach for multi-source image fusion.md>)) — yet still evaluate within one region and year, so they too stop short of transfer.

Even the internal accuracy estimates of classical methods inherit this flaw. Random Forest's out-of-bag error is unbiased only under the i.i.d. assumption Breiman invoked ([Breiman 2001](<breiman2001_RandomForests.md>)); for spatially autocorrelated remote-sensing data it overstates transfer, a subtlety the canonical RF review endorses with only a soft caveat ([Belgiu & Drăguț 2016](<Random forest in remote sensing: A review of applications and future.md>)). Tellingly, that same review is the rare classical source to document spatial non-transferability directly, citing studies in which RF accuracy fell when models were applied to new study areas and one in which a model was simply "not transferable" ([Belgiu & Drăguț 2016](<Random forest in remote sensing: A review of applications and future.md>)).

A second, compounding pitfall is the class-imbalance artifact. Crop datasets are dominated by a majority class, and overall accuracy can look excellent while minority-crop recall collapses. The cleanest illustrations report a wide gap between overall accuracy and macro-F1 — a U-Net at 95% overall accuracy but 74% F1, with intercrop classes near 15–25% ([U-Net Kenya CV4A](<Satellite Imagery Analysis for Crop Type Segmentation Using U-Net Architecture.md>)) — or note that RF "tends to favor the most representative classes" under imbalance ([Belgiu & Drăguț 2016](<Random forest in remote sensing: A review of applications and future.md>)). Studies that lead with balanced metrics — macro-F1, per-class F1, Cohen's Kappa, or average accuracy — are the minority but the more trustworthy: examples include weighted-loss segmentation with macro-F1 primary ([Rustowicz et al. 2019](<rustowicz2019_CropType_Africa.md>)), mIoU preferred precisely because four classes held 90% of samples ([Garnot & Landrieu](<ltae2020_Garnot_LTAE.md>)), average accuracy used to expose minority collapse ([Cropformer 2023](<cropformer2023_Wang.md>)), and recall-and-kappa-led rare-event forecasting that explicitly disclaims overall accuracy ([Mann et al., Ethiopia](<Predicting high-magnitude, low-frequency crop losses using machine learning: an application to cereal crops in Ethiopia.md>)). A worrying alternative practice is to engineer imbalance away by deleting minority classes and undersampling majorities before reporting, so the metrics describe an artificially balanced subset rather than native performance ([attention-BiLSTM + TempCNN](<bandar2024_BiLSTM_TCN.md>)).

The decisive question — does in-region ranking survive spatial transfer? — is answered by only a handful of studies, and their answer is sobering. A hyperspectral cross-domain study is the strongest single piece of evidence: a 3D-CNN scoring 97–99% in-region collapsed to "very poor" when its parameters were applied without retraining to disjoint, cross-sensor scenes, while a sparse SVM-plus-spatial-kernel method with a handful of parameters transferred successfully ([Pankajakshan et al., deep architectures fail to generalize](<Deep Architectures Fail to Generalize: A Lightweight Alternative for Agricultural Domain Transfer in Hyperspectral Images.md>)). In smallholder Africa, the deep-net advantage over RF that held in data-rich Germany evaporated or reversed — RF matched or beat the best deep nets on Ghanaian and South Sudanese smallholder data ([Rustowicz et al. 2019](<rustowicz2019_CropType_Africa.md>)). National-scale mapping that validated out-of-sample across landscapes exposed transfer penalties an in-region split would have hidden, with maize accuracy dropping from 79% in Tanzania to 63% in Kenya ([Jin et al., GEE smallholder maize](<Smallholder maize area and yield mapping at national scales with Google Earth Engine.md>)). Even transfer-learning CNN studies that guard against same-mosaic leakage concede that geographic and temporal generalization remains "not fully addressed" ([Nowakowski et al., transfer-learning crop mapping](<Crop type mapping by using transfer learning.md>)). Broad DL surveys state the same conclusion qualitatively: transfer typically fails under shifts in sensor, region, or acquisition time ([Ball et al. 2017](<ball2017_DL_RemoteSensing_survey.md>); [DL crops aerial-imagery review](<Deep Learning Models for the Classification of Crops in Aerial Imagery: A Review.md>)).

The one genuine counter-current is Cropformer, the corpus's clearest example of a dense architecture transferring well: a self-supervised pre-trained transformer-convolution model retained ~62–64% accuracy under cross-region transfer where RF collapsed to ~25% ([Cropformer 2023](<cropformer2023_Wang.md>)). This complicates any blanket claim that dense nets cannot transfer — but the result is confounded by 781k-sample self-supervised pre-training that ordinary supervised dense nets lack, suggesting that a strong learned prior, not architectural capacity per se, is what bought the transfer.

## 6. Knowledge gaps and future directions

The dominant gap is the scarcity of genuine spatial- and temporal-transfer evaluation. The overwhelming majority of crop-classification papers measure in-distribution fit and remain silent on transfer, often while invoking transfer as motivation ([U-Net Kenya CV4A](<Satellite Imagery Analysis for Crop Type Segmentation Using U-Net Architecture.md>); [Teixeira et al.](<Deep Learning Applications for Crop Mapping Using Multi-Temporal Sentinel-2 Data and Red-Edge Vegetation Indices: Integrating Convolutional and Recurrent Neural Networks.md>)) — a silence that is itself a finding. Domain adaptation and transfer learning are promising but under-validated for geographic shift specifically, with most "transfer" experiments testing on distribution-matched holdouts rather than truly disjoint terrain ([Nowakowski et al.](<Crop type mapping by using transfer learning.md>); [Cropformer 2023](<cropformer2023_Wang.md>)). Optical-SAR fusion needs transfer-validated rather than in-scene evidence, given that its benefits are scale-dependent and may not survive to small fields ([Rustowicz et al. 2019](<rustowicz2019_CropType_Africa.md>); [optical-SAR phenology fusion](<Crop classification based on phenology information by using time series of optical and synthetic-aperture radar images.md>)). Interpretability is a further open front: explainable-AI methods in remote sensing are evaluated largely anecdotally and often ignore the per-band, spatially autocorrelated structure of the data ([Höhl et al., XAI in RS](<hohl2024_XAI_RemoteSensing.md>)), even as sparse-attention mechanisms such as sparsemax offer a principled route to compact, interpretable feature selection ([Martins & Astudillo 2016](<From Softmax to Sparsemax - A Sparse Model of Attention and Multi-Label Classification.md>); [Arık & Pfister](<tabnet_orig_Arik.md>)). Finally, data efficiency and computational cost — how little labeled data and compute a transferable model needs — are rarely characterized despite their operational importance in the Global South ([Pankajakshan et al.](<Deep Architectures Fail to Generalize: A Lightweight Alternative for Agricultural Domain Transfer in Hyperspectral Images.md>); [Bégué et al.](<begue2020_RS_Africa_policy.md>)).

## 7. Positioning of the present study

The Western Cape study reported in the accompanying manuscript is positioned precisely in the spatial-transfer gap identified above. It benchmarks nineteen classical, deep-learning, and patch-based models on multi-temporal Sentinel-2 optical imagery (deliberately SAR-free, to isolate the spectral-temporal signal and maximize applicability to optical-only contexts), evaluating each model *twice*: under conventional in-region field-wise cross-validation, and on a spatially disjoint holdout tile (`34S_20E_259N`) adjacent to the training tiles but sharing no pixel or field. Because the holdout lies within the same agroecological zone, the reported degradation is a conservative lower bound on cross-region transfer loss. The two protocols produce sharply different conclusions: dense temporal deep nets top the in-region ranking (macro-F1 0.72–0.78), yet collapse under spatial transfer, with the CNN-BiLSTM ensemble — the very model conventional field-level evaluation singles out as best — suffering the largest degradation of any model (macro-F1 0.72 → 0.46) and falling to near-worst. The ranking inverts: a TabNet ensemble becomes the best single model (macro-F1 0.60, Kappa 0.54), and parsimonious classical baselines — gradient-boosted trees and even logistic regression — transfer with the smallest gaps. This directly extends the rare prior transfer studies ([Pankajakshan et al.](<Deep Architectures Fail to Generalize: A Lightweight Alternative for Agricultural Domain Transfer in Hyperspectral Images.md>); [Rustowicz et al. 2019](<rustowicz2019_CropType_Africa.md>); [Cropformer 2023](<cropformer2023_Wang.md>)) by re-ranking a broad model set on a common spatially disjoint holdout — something the literature has rarely done.

The study's interpretive contribution is mechanistic. It argues that the sparse, axis-aligned feature selection behind the two-decade dominance of Random Forest and gradient-boosted trees ([Breiman 2001](<breiman2001_RandomForests.md>); [Friedman](<GREEDY FUNCTION APPROXIMATION:.md>); [Chen & Guestrin 2016](<xgboost_chen2016.md>); [Belgiu & Drăguț 2016](<Random forest in remote sensing: A review of applications and future.md>)) is precisely the inductive bias that governs spatial transfer, and that TabNet inherits it through attentive sparsemax masks ([Arık & Pfister](<tabnet_orig_Arik.md>); [Martins & Astudillo 2016](<From Softmax to Sparsemax - A Sparse Model of Attention and Multi-Label Classification.md>)) while dense CNN, RNN, and attention temporal networks discard it and overfit region-specific signal ([Garnot & Landrieu](<ltae2020_Garnot_LTAE.md>); [Pelletier et al.](<Temporal Convolutional Neural Network for the Classification of Satellite Image Time Series.md>); [Rußwurm & Körner](<Multi-Temporal Land Cover Classification with Sequential Recurrent Encoders.md>)). It identifies field-level aggregation as a second, substitutable variance-reduction lever that recovers transfer for dense models but is redundant for already-sparse ones, shows via a training-set reduction experiment that the most transferable models retain near-full holdout accuracy with as little as 25% of training fields, and provides the first crop-classification evaluation of `xr_fresh` automated time-series feature extraction. The central message — that model selection in crop mapping is an artifact of the validation protocol, and that spatial transfer rather than in-distribution accuracy must drive it — generalizes the corpus-wide evaluation critique developed in Section 5 into a concrete, quantified prescription.
