# Crop type mapping by using transfer learning

**Citation:** Nowakowski, A., Mrziglod, J., Spiller, D., Bonifacio, R., Ferrari, I., Mathieu, P. P., Garcia-Herranz, M., & Kim, D.-H. (2021). Crop type mapping by using transfer learning. *International Journal of Applied Earth Observation and Geoinformation*, 98, 102313. DOI: 10.1016/j.jag.2021.102313
**BibTeX key:** `nowakowski2021type`

## Objectives

The paper addresses crop type mapping in data-scarce developing-country settings (Malawi and Mozambique) using high-resolution RGB drone imagery. The central problem is that deep CNNs need very large labelled datasets, which are unavailable for these field campaigns. The authors propose supervised transfer learning (TL): take CNNs pre-trained on ImageNet (`VGG16`, `GoogLeNet`), replace the final classification layer with a 6-output layer, and fine-tune on small drone crop datasets. A secondary objective is a parametric study of how many CNN layers should be frozen vs. fine-tuned, comparing the two architectures and the no-TL baseline.

## Methods

Input data are orthomosaics from drone campaigns: Malawi (Kasungu, May 2018; 40 mosaics, ~4 cm ground resolution, 5 crop classes — Cassava, Groundnut, Maize, Sweet potatoes, Tobacco, plus an "other" class for fallows and minority crops) and Mozambique (Gaza, spring 2019; 30 mosaics, ~1.3 cm resolution, 3 crops — Cassava, Maize, Rice, plus "other"). Image patches of `224 x 224 x 3` are sampled at 1000 random points per crop class within field polygons. The classifier is patch-based RGB only — no temporal sequence, no multispectral bands, no SAR. Crops are discriminated by visual/textural pattern (plant rows, canopy texture, color) rather than spectral signature, since the inputs are 3-band RGB. Class imbalance in the field count is handled by sampling an equal number of training patches per class; validation uses a proportional (real-distribution) sample. Heavy data augmentation (flip, rotation, translation, scaling) compensates for low polygon counts. Training uses SGD with momentum, 20 epochs, fixed hyperparameters. The TL knob studied is the number of frozen layers (0, 1, 4, … up to all-but-last), evaluated on both `GoogLeNet` and `VGG16`.

**Evaluation protocol:** Within-region hold-out by mosaic. For Malawi, training patches come from 36 mosaics and validation from the remaining 4 (26 train / 4 validation for Mozambique). The authors explicitly select disjoint mosaics for train vs. validation "to tackle the problem of spatial autocorrelation arising in closely distributed features" — so patches from the same orthomosaic are not split across train and test, a real and deliberate guard against same-field leakage. However, this is a single-region, single-season fixed hold-out, NOT a spatially disjoint transfer to an independent landscape, and NOT k-fold cross-validation (the authors note they avoided k-fold precisely so feature distributions would match across the comparison). Critically, the test mosaics were chosen so the feature distribution matches the training distribution ("a fixed test dataset has been chosen in order to have a distribution of the features similar to the one of the training dataset"). This is in-region validation, not out-of-distribution generalization. Metrics: macro-averaged overall accuracy, precision, recall, F1, AND weighted versions, plus per-class confusion matrices.

## Key Findings

Transfer learning works: fine-tuned ImageNet CNNs reach up to ~83% (Malawi) and ~90% (Mozambique) weighted overall accuracy; VGG16 F1 > 0.85 in the best Malawi configuration. Training CNNs from scratch (random weights) failed entirely — learning curves never converged — confirming that on these small datasets TL is what makes deep CNNs usable at all. The frozen-layer study found that fewer frozen layers is better: freezing 0–1–2 layers gives the best validation scores, and performance degrades monotonically as more early layers are frozen. The two architectures (`VGG16`, `GoogLeNet`) performed similarly. There is a large gap between weighted and macro-averaged scores on the imbalanced Malawi dataset: weighted precision stays high (~0.85) while macro recall is much lower (~0.5), and ~25% of plots are not properly recognized at the best operating point. Minority classes (Groundnut, Sweet potatoes, Tobacco) generalize worse than majority classes (Maize) because heterogeneous within-class variability is undersampled. Confusion matrices show groundnut frequently confused with sweet potatoes and tobacco. The better Mozambique results are attributed to a more balanced class distribution.

## Relevance to Our Crop-Classification Study

This paper is a useful contrast and a partial cautionary tale. On the supportive side, it directly demonstrates two of our themes: (1) dense deep nets cannot train from scratch on small agricultural datasets and collapse without inductive priors (here supplied by ImageNet pre-training) — analogous to our finding that dense temporal/patch nets overfit our training region; and (2) the macro-vs-weighted gap is large under class imbalance, exactly the artifact our study foregrounds (lucerne/medics majority). The authors' explicit mosaic-disjoint split shows good awareness of spatial autocorrelation, which mirrors our FID-disjoint splitting discipline.

On the cautionary side, this paper is the kind of in-region evaluation our thesis warns about. The test mosaics were deliberately chosen to match the training feature distribution, and live in the same region and season — so the headline 83–90% accuracies measure interpolation, not the spatially disjoint transfer our holdout tile measures. By the logic of our paper, these numbers would likely reorder (and drop) under a true out-of-region test. The paper itself acknowledges this in its discussion: it states that crop mapping generalization to "different geographical areas or temporal intervals" is "not fully addressed" and that even standard k-fold would not answer the spatial-correlation question. That admission is a direct endorsement of our evaluation argument. The work also differs from ours in design: RGB-only, spatial-texture-based, single-date (no temporal sequence, no `B6`/`B11`/`B12`/`EVI`/`hue`), patch-level granularity. It tells us nothing about temporal-sequence models or our specific band set, but it reinforces that field/patch aggregation plus imbalance-aware metrics are essential.

## Evaluation Caveats

- In-region validation disguised as the generalization question: test mosaics are same-region, same-season, and explicitly distribution-matched to training. No spatially disjoint holdout, no cross-year, no cross-sensor transfer. The headline accuracies are interpolation scores.
- Spatial leakage is partially controlled (train/validation use disjoint mosaics, preventing same-field patch leakage), but adjacency between nearby mosaics within a small campaign area is not ruled out, and the deliberate distribution-matching of the test set actively works against measuring transfer.
- Class imbalance is severe (Malawi dominated by maize/fallows). The authors do report macro-F1, per-class precision/recall, and confusion matrices alongside weighted scores — good practice — and the weighted/macro gap exposes that ~25% of fields and minority classes are poorly classified despite high weighted accuracy. Do not cite the weighted overall accuracy as the transfer result.
- Sensor/feature design is narrow: optical RGB only, no red-edge, SWIR, or vegetation indices, no SAR, and no multi-temporal information. Discrimination relies on spatial texture, so conclusions do not transfer cleanly to spectral-temporal pipelines like ours.
- Compute and minority-class robustness across truly new regions are not measured; the authors explicitly flag spatial-correlation analysis as out of scope and future work.
