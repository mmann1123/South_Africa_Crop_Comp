# MiniRocket: A Very Fast (Almost) Deterministic Transform for Time Series Classification

**Citation:** Angus Dempster, Daniel F. Schmidt, and Geoffrey I. Webb (2021). *Proceedings of the 27th ACM SIGKDD Conference on Knowledge Discovery & Data Mining (KDD '21)*, pp. 248-257. DOI: 10.1145/3447548.3467231 (verified against Crossref: title, authors, venue, year and pages match).
**Code:** https://github.com/angus924/minirocket (also implemented in `sktime` and `aeon` as `MiniRocket` / `MiniRocketMultivariate`).

## Objectives

MiniRocket is a reformulation of ROCKET (Dempster, Petitjean and Webb, 2020), a time-series-classification (TSC) method that transforms each input series with a large bank of random convolutional kernels, pools each kernel's output into a scalar feature, and trains a plain linear classifier on the result. The paper sets out to show that almost all of ROCKET's randomness can be removed, and that the resulting fixed kernel set can be computed far faster, without losing accuracy.

The explicit goal is not to maximize accuracy per se but to improve the accuracy-per-compute frontier. The authors argue that the most accurate TSC methods of the time (HIVE-COTE and its variants, TS-CHIEF, InceptionTime) require days to weeks of compute on the UCR archive, whereas a near-deterministic transform plus a linear model can reach statistically indistinguishable accuracy in minutes.

The paper is a general-purpose TSC methods paper. It contains no remote-sensing data, no crops, no spatial structure, and no domain-transfer experiment. It is relevant to us as a candidate "recent time-series baseline" (reviewer request; see `experiments/recent_baselines/`) and as a conceptual bridge between engineered time-series statistics (`xr_fresh`) and learned deep temporal encoders.

## Methods

**The transform.** Each univariate input series `X` is convolved with a fixed set of 84 kernels of length 9. Every kernel has six weights equal to `alpha = -1` and three equal to `beta = 2` (all `C(9,3) = 84` placements of the three `beta` values). Because the weights sum to zero, the convolution output is invariant to adding a constant to the input, so the kernels respond only to relative shape, not absolute level. The scale of `alpha`, `beta` is irrelevant because biases are drawn from the convolution output itself.

**Dilation.** Each kernel is applied at a fixed, exponentially spaced set of dilations `D = {floor(2^0), ..., floor(2^max)}` with `max = log2((l_input - 1)/(l_kernel - 1))`, so the widest dilated kernel spans the whole series. More features are allocated to small dilations. The number of dilations per kernel is capped at 32 by default.

**Bias and pooling.** For each kernel/dilation pair, bias values are taken as quantiles (e.g. 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, assigned by a low-discrepancy sequence) of the convolution output on a single randomly chosen training example. The only pooling is PPV, the "proportion of positive values": `PPV(X * W - b) = mean([X * W > b])`. The authors note that PPV is essentially an empirical CDF evaluated at the bias, so each feature is "what fraction of the season does this local shape pattern exceed threshold `b`". ROCKET's second pooled feature (global max) is dropped because it no longer helps.

**Feature count.** By default 9,996 features (nearest multiple of 84 below 10,000). The only stochastic element is the choice of training example used to sample bias quantiles. A fully deterministic variant (`MiniRocketDV`) draws the biases from the convolution output over the whole training set.

**Computational tricks.** (1) PPV for `W` gives PPV for `-W` for free (`PPV(-C) = 1 - PPV(C)`), so only half the kernels are computed. (2) One convolution output is reused for several bias values. (3) Two-valued weights let multiplications be factored out (`alpha*X` and `beta*X` are precomputed once; each convolution is a sum of shifted copies). (4) Since `beta = alpha + 3`, a shared output `C_alpha` is computed once per dilation and each kernel only adds its three shifted `3X` rows, so roughly two-thirds of the work is shared across all 84 kernels. Complexity remains `O(k * n * l_input)` (features x examples x length), i.e. linear in everything, with a memory overhead of about 13 extra copies of a single series.

**Classifier.** A ridge-regression classifier (`RidgeClassifierCV` in scikit-learn, with cross-validated regularization) for small datasets; logistic regression trained with Adam when there are more training examples than features (roughly above 10,000 examples). There is no hidden layer and no nonlinearity between the pooled features and the classifier, so the model is a direct linear function of PPV features.

**Multivariate data.** The paper itself is univariate. It mentions only a "naive facility" for multivariate series in the repository (in that code, and in `sktime`'s `MiniRocketMultivariate`, each kernel/dilation is applied to a random subset of channels and the channel outputs are summed before PPV). More principled multivariate handling is listed as future work.

**Data.** The UCR univariate archive: 109 datasets, 30 resamples each (the same resamples as recent HIVE-COTE/TDE/CIF papers), plus the three largest UCR datasets (`MosquitoSound` 139,780 train series of length 3,750; `InsectSound` 25,000 of length 600; `FruitFlies` 17,259 of length 5,000) for scaling, and `DucksAndGeese` (length 236,784) for series-length scaling. Hyperparameters were tuned on the same 40 "development" datasets as ROCKET to limit overfitting to the archive.

**Baselines.** HIVE-COTE/TDE, TS-CHIEF, InceptionTime, ROCKET, TDE, CIF, cBOSS, Proximity Forest. Compute is reported on a single CPU core (Xeon E5-2680 v3/4 and Gold 6150).

**Evaluation protocol:** Benchmark-archive accuracy only. Each UCR dataset has a fixed train/test split plus 29 stratified resamples drawn from the pooled train+test examples, and results are averaged over resamples and summarized as mean ranks with a Friedman test followed by Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Holm correction. On our spectrum this is the equivalent of pooled random resampling within one distribution: every UCR dataset is a single i.i.d. sample, so there is no notion of field, region, tile, year, or sensor, and nothing in the protocol measures distribution shift or transfer. Per rule 7, the paper is treated as a methods/benchmark paper, and its accuracy claims are read as in-distribution claims. The sensitivity analysis (kernel length, bias sampling, pooling, feature count, dilation cap) uses the 40 development datasets with default splits and 10 runs.

## Key Findings

**Accuracy.** Over 30 resamples of 109 UCR datasets, MiniRocket is on average marginally more accurate than ROCKET and slightly, but not statistically significantly, less accurate than the then-best methods (TS-CHIEF and HIVE-COTE/TDE). Pairwise against ROCKET it wins on 61 datasets, draws on 3, and loses on 45, mostly by small margins. The one large loss (`PigAirwayPressure`) is attributed to bias sampling.

**Determinism.** The deterministic variant `MiniRocketDV` gives essentially the same accuracy as the default (win/draw/loss 42/11/56 for default vs DV), demonstrating that ROCKET-level accuracy does not require random kernels.

**Speed.** Total compute for all 109 datasets on one CPU core: about 8 minutes for MiniRocket (2 min 30 s of transform) versus 2 h 2 min for ROCKET. By comparison (hardware not identical) cBOSS takes about 13 h, CIF over a day, TDE over two days, Proximity Forest about a week, HIVE-COTE over two weeks, TS-CHIEF several weeks, and InceptionTime (on GPUs, original splits only) over 4 days. On the large datasets MiniRocket is 43x (`InsectSound`), 66x (`FruitFlies`) and 75x (`MosquitoSound`, 12 min 32 s vs 15 h 35 min) faster than ROCKET at near-identical accuracy (e.g. `MosquitoSound` 0.8165 vs 0.8271). On `DucksAndGeese` it is about 19x faster.

**Sensitivity.** Kernel length 9 is best but 7 and 11 are not significantly worse; the 84-kernel `9{3}` subset is nearly as good as all 512 length-9 two-valued kernels. Sampling biases from the convolution output instead of `U(-1, 1)` is critical; with uniform biases MiniRocket is significantly worse. Adding global max pooling to PPV does not help (slightly worse, not significant). Accuracy rises with feature count up to about 10,000 and plateaus beyond, with the authors noting there is little benefit in computing PPV at many more than `l_input` bias values for short series. The dilation cap matters little between 16 and 119.

**Authors' conclusion.** MiniRocket should be the default variant of ROCKET; it is "significantly faster than any other method of comparable accuracy" and "significantly more accurate than any other method of remotely similar computational expense". Future work: better multivariate handling, nonlinear classifiers on MiniRocket features, and non-time-series data.

## Relevance to Our Crop-Classification Study

**Why it is in the corpus.** Reviewers asked us to compare against recent time-series baselines rather than only TempCNN, L-TAE and CNN-BiLSTM. MiniRocket (with ROCKET and MultiRocket) is the standard fast, strong, non-deep reference point in the TSC literature and a fixture of the "bake off redux" benchmark also in this folder. It occupies a methodologically distinctive slot: a temporal-convolution feature extractor with no learned convolutional weights, followed by a linear head. That makes it an informative probe of our central claim that inductive bias, not capacity, decides whether a model survives spatial transfer.

**Random/fixed kernels + PPV + ridge as a sparse/linear head.** Our manuscript argues that models with sparse, axis-aligned feature selection (gradient-boosted trees, RF, TabNet via `sparsemax`, our `L-TAE-S`) are more stable on the holdout tile, while dense temporal deep nets that learn their own representation overfit the training region. MiniRocket sits between these camps in an interesting way. Its representation is not learned at all, so it cannot co-adapt features to region-specific idiosyncrasies of `34S_19E_258N`/`34S_19E_259N`, which is the failure mode we attribute to CNN-BiLSTM and the pixel Transformer. On the other hand, its head is dense rather than sparse: `RidgeClassifierCV` applies an L2 penalty, so every one of the ~10,000 PPV features gets a nonzero weight, and nothing in the model performs axis-aligned selection. MiniRocket is therefore a clean test of which half of our mechanism matters. If it transfers about as well as XGBoost or TabNet, that suggests a fixed, non-learned temporal representation plus heavy regularization is enough, and that "not learning region-specific representations" is the operative property. If it falls with the dense deep nets, it supports the stronger claim that explicit sparse selection is the lever. A natural variant that sharpens the test is to swap the ridge head for an L1-penalized logistic regression (or feed PPV features to XGBoost/TabNet), turning MiniRocket into a sparse-selection model on the same features. The paper itself lists nonlinear classifiers on MiniRocket features as future work, so this is a defensible extension rather than an ad hoc tweak.

**Analogy to `xr_fresh` features.** MiniRocket is best understood as an automatically generated feature-engineering library. Each PPV feature is a quantile-thresholded occurrence frequency of a local, mean-invariant shape pattern (a zero-sum kernel at a given dilation), which is conceptually close to the hand-designed `xr_fresh` statistics we compute per band (e.g. counts above/below mean, quantiles, change statistics, autocorrelation-like terms). Both produce a large, fixed-length, order-free vector of summary statistics per series and both hand it to a downstream learner. The difference is that `xr_fresh` features are few, interpretable and chosen by a domain expert, whereas MiniRocket's are many, opaque, and chosen by a generic rule. Our controlled XGBoost experiment (raw monthly reflectance vs `xr_fresh` statistics; Table `tab:repr`) found that representation mattered less than model family under transfer. MiniRocket offers a third representation arm for that comparison: generic shape statistics, fed to the same XGBoost, would test whether `xr_fresh`'s operational value comes from domain knowledge or just from being a summary-statistic transform. One caution: because the kernels sum to zero, MiniRocket features are blind to absolute band level (only relative shape within the season is encoded). For crop discrimination, absolute reflectance and index magnitudes (e.g. peak `EVI`) carry signal that `xr_fresh` keeps and MiniRocket discards; this may hurt in-region accuracy but could plausibly help transfer if the holdout tile has an offset in radiometry or phenology timing that is level-shifted rather than shape-shifted.

**Applying it to multivariate Sentinel-2 series.** Our sequences are short and multivariate: 6 channels (`B2`, `B6`, `B11`, `B12`, `EVI`, `hue`) by 10 monthly time steps (months 05 and 06 excluded; `T_SEQ = 10`, `N_BANDS = 6` in `experiments/field_reduction/models_arch.py`). This is far outside the regime MiniRocket was tuned on (UCR series are typically hundreds to thousands of steps). Several consequences follow directly from the paper's own design. First, with `l_input = 10` and `l_kernel = 9`, the dilation exponent `max = log2(9/8)` is about 0.17, so only dilation 1 is available; the multi-scale dilation that the authors identify as one of ROCKET's two most important ingredients collapses entirely. Second, with padding a length-9 kernel essentially spans the whole season, so the kernels act as near-global seasonal shape templates rather than local motif detectors. Third, the authors observe there is little value in many more than `l_input` bias values per kernel, so the default ~10,000 features will be massively redundant on a 10-step series (and highly collinear, which ridge tolerates but which inflates the feature-to-sample ratio). Reasonable choices are to reduce `num_kernels`/features substantially, to try ROCKET (random lengths 7/9/11, random dilation, plus max pooling) or MultiRocket, and possibly to interpolate to a denser time axis, while acknowledging that interpolation adds no information. For the multivariate case, `MiniRocketMultivariate` sums kernel outputs over random channel subsets; an alternative more in keeping with our sparse-selection thesis is to apply univariate MiniRocket per band and concatenate, keeping features axis-aligned to bands so a sparse head can select them.

**Pixel vs field level.** MiniRocket is cheap enough to run per pixel on our whole training set, then aggregate predictions to fields by majority vote or by mean-pooled ridge decision scores, matching how our pixel-level L-TAE/TempCNN are scored. Alternatively, features can be computed per pixel and mean-pooled within each FID before fitting (field-level, analogous to `final_data.parquet`), or the transform can be applied to the field-mean series directly. Because PPV is a nonlinear (threshold) function, "mean of PPV over pixels" differs from "PPV of mean series"; the former retains within-field variability and is closer to our pixel-then-aggregate models. Our finding that field-level aggregation is a second, substitutable lever for transfer suggests the field-level variant should be reported alongside the pixel one. Splits must remain FID-wise (`random_state=42`) with the shared split helpers in `experiments/recent_baselines/common.py`; the single training example used for bias sampling should be drawn from training FIDs only, and the fitted transform must be frozen before touching `34S_20E_259N`.

**Compute cost.** This is MiniRocket's strongest practical argument and directly relevant to an operational Global-South framing. The transform is linear in examples, features and length, runs on CPU, and needs no GPU or architecture search. On our 10-step series the per-example transform cost is tiny; the dominant cost will be the ridge fit on an `n_pixels x ~10,000` matrix, where the paper's own guidance (switch to logistic regression with SGD/Adam above ~10,000 examples) applies, since our pixel-level training set is far larger than that. A field-level fit is trivially cheap. For the manuscript, MiniRocket gives a useful compute anchor: a model trained in minutes on a laptop CPU, against which the multi-seed TabNet, L-TAE-S and patch-CNN ensembles can be weighed.

**Class imbalance.** The paper reports only accuracy and mean ranks, and `RidgeClassifierCV` has no class weighting by default. With lucerne/medics at 43% of training fields and 56% of the holdout, an unweighted ridge head will drift toward the majority class; use `class_weight="balanced"` (supported by `RidgeClassifierCV`) and report macro-F1, per-class F1 and Kappa as for every other model.

## Evaluation Caveats

Every accuracy number in the paper is an in-distribution number: UCR resamples pool and re-split each dataset's examples, so train and test come from the same distribution and there is no analogue of field-wise splitting, spatial separation, or cross-year/cross-sensor transfer. The paper says nothing about robustness to domain shift, which is exactly the axis on which our study reorders models; its "state of the art" ranking should not be cited as evidence that MiniRocket will transfer well to a holdout tile.

Accuracy is the only metric. There is no balanced metric (macro-F1, per-class recall, Kappa), even though many UCR datasets are imbalanced, so the paper offers no evidence on minority-class behavior. This matters for us given the lucerne/medics majority.

The design and hyperparameter defaults (kernel length 9, ~10,000 features, dilation cap 32, quantile bias sampling) were tuned on 40 UCR development datasets that are overwhelmingly univariate and much longer than our 10-step monthly series. Several of the mechanisms the authors identify as critical (exponential dilation, many bias quantiles per kernel) degenerate on a series of length 10, so the paper's accuracy claims do not directly carry over and its defaults should be treated as starting points.

Multivariate handling is explicitly naive and unevaluated in the paper. Any result on our 6-band input therefore tests a configuration (`MiniRocketMultivariate` or per-band concatenation) that the paper did not validate.

Compute comparisons across methods are, by the authors' own admission, only broadly comparable (different hardware, software, single core vs GPU, resamples vs original splits). The MiniRocket-vs-ROCKET speedups are like-for-like; the comparisons to HIVE-COTE, TS-CHIEF and InceptionTime are indicative.

What the paper does not measure: spatial or temporal transfer, per-class performance, calibration of the ridge scores (relevant if we want probabilities for ensemble voting or the cross-entropy metric used by AI4FoodSecurity), interpretability of PPV features, and behavior on short, irregular or cloud-gapped series typical of optical satellite time series.
