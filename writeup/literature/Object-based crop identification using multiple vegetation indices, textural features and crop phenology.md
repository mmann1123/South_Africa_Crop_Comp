## Objectives

Develop and evaluate an Object-based Crop Identification and Mapping (OCIM) methodology that couples object-based image analysis (OBIA) with decision-tree (DT) classification to map a large number of crop types and their within-field status across a Mediterranean agricultural landscape (Yolo County, California). Three explicit objectives: (1) explore relationships between multiple vegetation indices (VIs) and textural features and the studied crops at parcel scale; (2) evaluate the contribution of visible, near-infrared (NIR) and short-wave infrared (SWIR) spectral regions to crop and management-practice identification; (3) determine the influence of different growing-season periods (mid-spring, early-summer, late-summer) on crop discrimination.

## Methods

Sensor: ASTER (visible green/red + NIR at 15 m; six SWIR bands at 30 m resampled to 15 m), three multi-date scenes in 2006 (May 7 mid-spring, June 24 early-summer, Sept 12 late-summer), atmospherically corrected to reflectance with FLAASH/MODTRAN4. Twenty VIs were computed per date (visible, visible–NIR, NIR–SWIR, visible–SWIR, SWIR-region families — including `NDVI`, `GNDVI`, `NDI7`, `NDSVI`, `NDTI`, `LCA`, `SINDRI`), engineered to capture greenness, canopy structure, water/moisture, non-photosynthetic vegetation and crop residue.

OCIM has two phases. (1) **Field delimitation by segmentation:** Definiens multiresolution segmentation builds a hierarchical object network matching parcel boundaries; segmentation parameters were optimized against 350 reference fields (94% averaged accuracy), producing a two-level hierarchy (tiny objects → 5–10 ha fields → >10 ha merged fields). (2) **Rule-based DT classification:** a feature space of **336 object features** in three groups — object spectral (mean and SD of each VI/band), object texture (GLCM homogeneity, dissimilarity, entropy — a Haralick subset), and sub-object texture (relative area of sub-objects below VI thresholds) — fed a recursive-partitioning DT (JMP 8, likelihood-ratio chi-square splits). The DT is the key transfer-relevant design choice: it is a sparse, feature-selecting, interpretable "white-box" model that intentionally sorts hundreds of candidate features down to a small subset of physically meaningful splits and thresholds, and accommodates intra-class variation via multiple phenological sub-classes per crop (e.g., Alfalfa_1–4, Tomato_1–6). Four scenarios were built (all three periods, or pairs) to assess each season's contribution.

**Evaluation protocol:** A three-way independent split of 1300 segmented fields drawn from ~20% of Yolo County — training and validation sets of n/4 each (with ten-replicate 50/50 random cross-validation for DT pruning/feature selection), and a held-out **independent test set of 650 fields (2n/4)**. Final accuracy is reported on the independent test set via a **field-scale confusion matrix** (overall and per-class producer's/user's accuracy, plus kappa). This is **within-region, field-level (object-level) holdout within a single county and single year (2006)**. It is genuinely field/object-disjoint (test fields are separate objects from training fields) and scored at field scale — strictly better than pooled-pixel k-fold — but it is **not** spatially disjoint (test fields are interspersed across the same county) and **not** cross-year or cross-sensor. On our protocol spectrum it is field-wise holdout within one scene/year; it does not measure geographic transfer.

## Key Findings

Three-period OCIM achieved **79% overall accuracy, kappa 0.75** (overall producer's 77%, user's 80%) on the 650-field independent test set across 13 major crops grouped into permanent crops, summer crops, winter cereals and meadows. DT cross-validation error rate was 9% for the three-period model, rising to 12/13/16% when mid-spring/early-summer/late-summer respectively were dropped — late-summer was the single most decisive period (intra-class variability peaks late, requiring the most features). **Spectral VIs contributed ~86–94% of model discrimination (NDVI alone ~50%), texture only 6–14%.** Texture was nonetheless necessary to separate spectrally similar permanent crops (orchards, vineyard, alfalfa, meadow) — GLCM homogeneity/dissimilarity resolved alfalfa↔walnut. SWIR-based VIs (`NDSVI`, `NDI7`, `NDTI`, `LCA`) were crucial for moisture, non-photosynthetic vegetation, residue and bare-soil discrimination. Late-summer features supplied ~60% of DT splits, mid-spring ~30%, early-summer ~10%. Per-crop accuracy ranged widely: rice 93–95% (flooded fields, distinctive NIR/SWIR water signal) and alfalfa/vineyard/corn/sunflower/meadow >80% producer's, down to almond and rye ~50–60% producer's (almond↔vineyard, rye↔meadow confusions). The model intentionally captured intra-class sub-classes tied to local crop calendars and management (tillage, residue, cutting schedules).

## Relevance to Our Crop-Classification Study

This is a strong supporting reference for the classical / tree-based, feature-selecting branch of our comparison and for our field-level aggregation strategy. Its core architecture — OBIA segmentation to field objects, then a sparse interpretable decision tree over many engineered spectral/textural/phenological features — is precisely the kind of "sparse, feature-selecting, tree-like" model our manuscript finds transfers best out-of-sample. The DT's deliberate reduction of 336 candidate features to a handful of physically meaningful splits is the mechanism we hypothesize underlies robust transfer (versus dense temporal/patch nets that overfit scene-specific texture). The paper also reinforces several of our feature/sensor choices: optical-only (no SAR); heavy reliance on SWIR bands (our `B11`,`B12`) and multi-temporal phenology; engineered VIs (overlapping our `EVI` and band-ratio logic) rather than raw pixels; and explicit field/object granularity matching our per-FID, field-level final predictions. Its finding that NDVI dominates but SWIR-residue indices are essential for senescent/non-photosynthetic discrimination directly supports our band selection rationale (`B2`,`B6`,`B11`,`B12`,`EVI`,`hue`). The use of multiple phenological sub-classes per crop to absorb intra-class calendar variation is a conceptual precedent for handling our lucerne/medics and small-grain-grazing heterogeneity.

Where it does **not** help us is on the validation question our manuscript foregrounds. The 79% OA / 0.75 kappa headline comes from a within-county, single-year field holdout, not a spatially disjoint distant tile. So this paper cannot itself tell us whether OCIM would survive geographic transfer — but our thesis predicts that, being a sparse feature-selecting DT scored at field level, it is exactly the model class that *should* transfer comparatively well, making it a useful positive control rather than a cautionary one. Cite it for the protocol (field-level independent holdout within one region/year), not for any claim of geographic robustness.

## Evaluation Caveats

- **No spatial disjointness:** Train/val/test fields are interspersed across the same county and the same 2006 acquisition. Field-disjoint but not space-disjoint and not year-disjoint — apparent accuracy benefits from shared regional climate, soils, sensor geometry, and a single crop calendar. The study never tests transfer to another region or year.
- **Field-level scoring (good):** Accuracy is reported on independent test *fields* via a field-scale confusion matrix, avoiding the pixel-autocorrelation inflation seen in pixel-wise studies — directly comparable to our field-level macro-F1 philosophy.
- **Balanced metric:** Reports kappa (0.75) alongside per-class producer's/user's accuracy and overall accuracy, partially controlling for class imbalance; however it does **not** report a macro-F1, and overall accuracy on a non-uniform 13-class set still favors common crops.
- **Single-year, single-sensor:** ASTER, 2006 only; the authors themselves flag that rules are calibrated to local crop calendars and "future analyses are necessary to evaluate robustness" across regions/years — an explicit acknowledgment of the transfer silence.
- **Best-year cherry-pick:** 2006 was chosen because cloud-free scenes covered the zone on all three key dates; cloud-robustness (a major issue in our and the smallholder literature) is sidestepped, not solved.
- **What it does not measure:** geographic transfer, cross-year/cross-sensor transfer, macro-F1, and performance under cloud contamination.
