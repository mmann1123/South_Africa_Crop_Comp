# Deep Architectures Fail to Generalize: A Lightweight Alternative for Agricultural Domain Transfer in Hyperspectral Images

**Citation:** Pankajakshan, P., Padmasanan, A., & Sundar, S. (2025). Deep Architectures Fail to Generalize: A Lightweight Alternative for Agricultural Domain Transfer in Hyperspectral Images. *Sensors*, 26(1), 174. DOI: 10.3390/s26010174

## Objectives

This is the single most on-point comparator in the corpus: a true cross-domain transfer study whose central thesis is identical in spirit to ours — that deep architectures achieving near-perfect closed-set accuracy fail to generalize to new domains, and that a sparse, low-parameter, inductive-bias-rich alternative transfers better. The authors target hyperspectral image (HSI) crop/land-cover classification under "open-set" agricultural conditions: class-distribution shift, novel classes absent from training, and known classes absent from the test region (`ω_train ∩ ω_test = ∅`). They propose a lightweight framework that explicitly balances spatial nearness against spectral similarity, is trained on closed benchmark datasets with only 1–10% labels and at most two tuneable parameters, and is then transferred WITHOUT retraining to unseen sensors, seasons, classes, and regions. The motivating application is paddy-rice extent mapping (food security and methane-emission monitoring).

## Methods

The pipeline: (1) project HSI pixels onto a lower-dimensional spectral manifold via PCA (explained-variance threshold ≥0.90, effectively 18–30 components); (2) train a pixel-wise SVM (RBF kernel, `ThunderSVM`, GPU) on 1–10% labelled pixels to produce an initial class-probability map; (3) iterative unsupervised refinement using a CRF-like energy that fuses a spatial-nearness Gaussian kernel `S_i` and a spectral-similarity Gaussian kernel `W_i`, trading them off with parameter `β`, optimized by an ICM-style site-update loop; (4) ensemble voting over four random feature subsets; (5) final weak labelling. The framework deliberately resembles localized self-attention (the kernels play the role of attention weights) but with only six parameters, four of which (`r`, `σ_w`, `σ_s`, `β`) are fixed and transferable across datasets, leaving only `C` (and optionally `γ`) to estimate per dataset. The authors emphasize sparse, tree/SVM-like inductive structure over dense deep representation, and note SVM associates spatially adjacent pixels with similar signatures better than tree-based or CNN classifiers while using fewer parameters. Sensor/feature design: full hyperspectral (171–224 bands reduced via PCA), pixel-level granularity with a local spatial neighborhood (`r=4`), single-date snapshots (no temporal sequence). The transfer test additionally fuses external evidence: Sentinel-1 VH for the flooded-rice mask and Sentinel-2 NDVI signatures for validation.

**Evaluation protocol:** Two distinct regimes, clearly separated. (A) Closed-set benchmark evaluation on Indian Pines, Salinas, Pavia University (AVIRIS/ROSIS), with deliberately reduced training fractions (1% Salinas/Pavia, 10% Indian Pines) to stress-test label scarcity; metrics are OA, Average Accuracy (AA — a balanced, per-class-averaged metric), and Kappa, with full per-class accuracy tables. Train/test pixels are split from the same scene (standard HSI practice — this regime DOES carry spatial-leakage risk from adjacent pixels, the usual HSI pitfall, and the authors do not claim otherwise for this part). (B) The load-bearing transfer regime: parameters fixed on one dataset, then the model is applied to genuinely different datasets and a real new region WITHOUT retraining. Specifically, train on Indian Pines, infer on Kennedy Space Center and Botswana (different AVIRIS/Hyperion scenes, different classes); and train on benchmarks, infer on PRISMA hyperspectral imagery over Piedmont, Italy (different sensor, different region, novel paddy class, `ω_train ∩ ω_study = ∅`). This is spatially disjoint, cross-sensor, cross-region, open-set transfer — the strongest generalization test in the corpus. PRISMA validation is by proxy (Sentinel-1 rice mask, Sentinel-2 NDVI signatures, ESA WorldCover, Street View) because no field campaign data existed, which the authors flag as a limitation.

## Key Findings

The headline result is the direct evidence for our thesis. The authors trained a 3D-CNN (spectral-spatial deep net) that reached 99.49% training and 97.24% validation accuracy on Indian Pines, then inferred it on the Barengo (Piedmont) study area — and "the model was NOT transferable and the results were very poor despite" the high in-region scores. Their lightweight SVM-plus-kernel method, by contrast, transferred successfully across all cross-domain tests without retraining: Salinas-A OA/AA 100%; Botswana spectral-spatial AA/κ rose to 97.4%/98.3%; KSC OA/κ rose to 97.1%/99.2%; and the PRISMA paddy map showed strong agreement with the independent Sentinel-1 rice mask. On the closed benchmarks the method is competitive with transformer SOTA (Salinas OA 99.42% after refinement; Indian Pines ~97%; matching/exceeding MASSFormer and SSRN) while using far fewer parameters, requiring 1–10% labels, training in <21 s on CPU (no GPU needed for the core), and gaining 7.22–15% OA over the spectral-only SVM baseline. Ablation: pixel-wise SVM alone is noisy and insufficient for open-set; CRF regularization improves homogeneity but can propagate wrong initial labels and cannot handle unknown classes; the full iterative weak-supervision pipeline is the most balanced and most robust to domain transfer and under-represented classes. The dominant confusion (Soybean-mintill vs. Corn-notill) is attributed to crop-residue from the prior season under minimum-tillage — a single-date snapshot limitation that multi-temporal data would resolve.

## Relevance to Our Crop-Classification Study

This is our strongest external corroboration and should be cited as such. Its core empirical claim — a 3D-CNN with 97–99% in-region accuracy collapses to "very poor" on a spatially disjoint, cross-sensor region, while a sparse low-parameter method transfers — is the exact phenomenon our study reports when we move from field-wise k-fold within the training tiles to the disjoint holdout tile (`34S_20E_259N`). It independently confirms three of our pillars: (1) in-region accuracy systematically misranks transferability — high closed-set scores do not survive domain shift; (2) sparse, feature-selecting, tree/kernel-like models with strong inductive bias and few parameters transfer best, while dense deep nets overfit the training domain "by design"; and (3) spatial regularization / aggregation (their CRF-style spatial-nearness term, analogous to our field-level FID aggregation) is a variance-reduction lever that recovers usable predictions. The shared evaluation philosophy — explicitly distinguishing closed-set benchmark scores from a true `ω_train ∩ ω_test = ∅` cross-region test, and refusing to let the former stand in for the latter — is precisely the methodological move our manuscript advocates. Differences worth noting: their inputs are hyperspectral single-date (vs. our multi-temporal Sentinel-2 with `B2,B6,B11,B12,EVI,hue`), they operate open-set (novel/absent classes) which we do not, and their refinement is unsupervised/iterative rather than supervised aggregation. Their explicit observation that single-date confusion (residue/tillage) would be resolved by multi-temporal data is a useful complement to our temporal design. Use this paper to anchor the claim that the deep-net-collapse-under-transfer finding is general across sensors, regions, and even the open-set setting, not an artifact of our particular tiles.

## Evaluation Caveats

- Two regimes must not be conflated. The closed-set benchmark numbers (Indian Pines/Salinas/Pavia) split train and test pixels from the SAME scene — the classic HSI spatial-leakage setting where adjacent train/test pixels inflate accuracy. Those numbers are not the transfer evidence; the cross-region/cross-sensor inference is.
- The transfer regime (the load-bearing part) has no ground-truth field campaign for PRISMA/Piedmont. Validation is by proxy (Sentinel-1 rice mask, Sentinel-2 NDVI, ESA WorldCover, Street View), so the strong transfer claim rests on inter-method agreement, not labelled accuracy. The authors flag this as a limitation; treat the PRISMA result as a qualitative feasibility demonstration, not a metric.
- Balanced metrics are reported well: AA and Kappa accompany OA on every benchmark, with full per-class tables — appropriate for the imbalanced HSI classes. The transfer regime, lacking labels, reports no balanced per-class metric for the new region.
- Sensor/feature design differs from ours: full hyperspectral (PCA-reduced) and single-date, pixel-level with a small spatial neighborhood — no multi-temporal sequence, no optical-band-subset design. The 3D-CNN failure is the most transferable lesson; the specific spectral pipeline is not directly comparable to our `B2,B6,B11,B12,EVI,hue` Sentinel-2 setup.
- The method is open-set / partially unsupervised, so its absolute scores are not directly comparable to our supervised macro-F1; cite the transfer protocol and the deep-net-collapse contrast, not the headline OA.
